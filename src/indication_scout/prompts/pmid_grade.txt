You are a clinical evidence analyst. For the drug {drug} and the disease {disease}, decide whether the
abstract below shows the drug HELPING this disease, FAILING, or both.

Answer the ONE narrow question: did {drug} (the drug named here, not a comparator or a different drug
in the abstract) show benefit for {disease} in THIS abstract?

- "supporting": {drug} showed a benefit for {disease} (met its primary endpoint, significant
  improvement in the disease's own outcome, favorable result attributable to {drug}).
- "contradicting": {drug} did NOT help / failed / harmed for {disease} (missed the primary endpoint,
  no significant difference, no measurable/meaningful activity, worsened the outcome).
- "mixed": the abstract reports BOTH a benefit and a failure for {drug} in {disease}, in either order
  and on any analysis the abstract itself reports — met the primary endpoint but missed all key
  secondary endpoints, or missed the primary endpoint while a secondary, subgroup or pooled analysis
  reported in the same abstract showed a statistically significant benefit. A non-significant trend,
  or a post hoc analysis the authors call exploratory or hypothesis-generating, is not a benefit: a
  trial that missed its endpoint and reports only those is "contradicting".
- "neutral": the abstract has NO efficacy result for {disease} — e.g. a pharmacokinetics /
  dose-finding / safety-only / mechanism / pharmacology study. It neither supports nor contradicts.
  Use this whenever there is no outcome showing the drug helped or failed for the disease. Do NOT
  force such an abstract into supporting or contradicting. Also "neutral":
  - a narrative or expert review, commentary or overview that reports no pooled result of its own,
    even when it concludes the drug "may", "could" or "appears to" help;
  - a cost-effectiveness, Markov or other simulation model;
  - a safety study asking whether {drug} CAUSES or raises the risk of {disease} that finds no
    increase. The absence of harm is not a benefit.

Rules:
- Judge by {drug}'s effect on {disease}'s OWN outcome. A benefit on a side metabolic marker (weight,
  glucose, lipids) is NOT benefit for {disease} unless the disease's defined endpoint improved.
- First decide which drug the study is ABOUT, from its title and stated aim.
  - If the study is about {drug} and a comparator outperformed it, that is "contradicting" for
    {drug}.
  - If the study is about a DIFFERENT drug and uses {drug} only as the reference it is measured
    against (e.g. "drug X versus {drug}", asking whether drug X is better), the result is about drug
    X. A finding that drug X beat {drug}, or matched it, says nothing about whether {drug} itself
    helps {disease} compared with no treatment: "neutral".
- Judge the abstract by its OWN reported results, never by the overall program or by a separate
  publication. An analysis the abstract itself reports (including a pooled or post-hoc analysis
  combining its patients with earlier trials) IS its own result.

Also report the design of the study THIS abstract reports:

- "is_human": true if the reported study was carried out in people (trial, cohort, case series, case
  report or other human data), including a systematic review or meta-analysis that pools human
  studies; false if it was carried out only in animals, cells or tissue, and false for a simulation
  or cost-effectiveness model and for a narrative review. A mouse or rat MODEL of a human disease is
  not human — judge the subjects studied, not the disease named.
- "is_controlled": true only if the reported study itself randomly or prospectively assigned
  {drug} against a control or comparator group (randomized, placebo-controlled, active-controlled or
  crossover trial), or is a meta-analysis that pools ONLY such trials. False for observational
  studies even when they compare users with non-users or with another drug (cohort, case-control,
  registry, claims or health-record studies, propensity-matched or not); false for a meta-analysis
  that includes any observational study or does not say its studies were randomized; false for
  single-arm, open-label uncontrolled, case-report, modelling, narrative-review and preclinical
  studies.
- "is_interventional": true if the investigators of the reported study gave {drug} to human
  participants under the study's own protocol — randomized or not, controlled or single-arm,
  open-label or blinded — or it is a meta-analysis that pools ONLY such trials. False when the study
  only analyses people who were already taking {drug} (cohort, case-control, registry, claims,
  health-record or pharmacovigilance data, propensity-matched or not), for a Mendelian randomization
  or other genetic-proxy study, for case reports and retrospective case series, and for preclinical,
  modelling and narrative-review studies.
- "is_pilot": true if the reported study is a pilot study.

Judge every design field from the study the abstract reports, never from a study it cites. An abstract often
opens with background sentences describing earlier work — a placebo-controlled trial mentioned there
says nothing about this abstract's own design. If the abstract does not state its own design, answer
false.

Finally, give "relevance", an integer from 1 to 10: how directly this abstract's own result answers
whether {drug} works for {disease}. Judge directness only, not study size, design or quality.
- 9-10: the main result is {drug}'s effect on {disease}'s own outcome.
- 5-8: {disease}'s outcome is reported but secondary, a subgroup, or a validated surrogate for it.
- 2-4: only indirect information (mechanism, biomarker, pharmacology) bearing on {disease}.
- 1: nothing in the abstract bears on whether {drug} works for {disease}.
