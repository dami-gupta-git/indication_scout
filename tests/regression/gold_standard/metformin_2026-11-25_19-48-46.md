## Drug Safety

_Drug-wide safety signal — applies to the drug generally, not to any single candidate indication below._

**Regulatory label:** FDA label boxed-warning text (17 product labels, LLM-summarized — see appendix for verbatim text):
All 17 labels agree that the core boxed-warning hazard is metformin-associated lactic acidosis, a serious and potentially fatal complication characterized by elevated blood lactate levels (>5 mmol/L), anion gap acidosis without ketonuria or ketonemia, an increased lactate/pyruvate ratio, and metformin plasma levels generally >5 mcg/mL, with postmarketing cases resulting in death, hypothermia, hypotension, and resistant bradyarrhythmias; onset is often subtle, with nonspecific symptoms including malaise, myalgias, respiratory distress, somnolence, and abdominal pain. Shared risk factors across nearly all labels include renal impairment, hepatic impairment, hypoxic states, excessive alcohol intake, radiological studies with contrast, and surgery or other procedures; management in all labels requires immediate discontinuation of metformin, hospitalization with general supportive measures, and prompt hemodialysis. Two substantive differences exist among the labels: the older-format labels (covering GLUCOPHAGE/GLUCOPHAGE XR, FORTAMET, GLUMETZA [older version], the unnamed metformin HCl USP generics, and the Glipizide/Metformin combination product) specify an age-based prescribing restriction — that metformin should not be initiated in patients ≥80 years of age unless creatinine clearance confirms preserved renal function — and characterize lactic acidosis incidence quantitatively (approximately 0.03 cases/1,000 patient-years, fatal in approximately 50% of cases), neither of which appears in the newer-format labels; additionally, ZITUVIMET and the newer Glumetza label uniquely add mitochondrial diseases as an explicit risk factor not listed in any other label, and the older unnamed GLUMETZA label (the shorter-format version) omits the specific laboratory thresholds, the lactate/pyruvate ratio, and the metformin plasma level criterion, instead describing laboratory abnormalities only as low pH, increased anion gap, and elevated blood lactate, making it the least detailed of all the boxed warnings reviewed.

## Findings by Disease

### Miscarriage

#### Literature — Miscarriage

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 7

The preponderance of evidence supports metformin in reducing miscarriage rates in women with PCOS. Multiple studies report significant reductions in early pregnancy loss with metformin continuation during the first trimester (PMIDs: 11163815, 17162710, 20441061), and a pooled individual participant data analysis from three RCTs found a significant reduction in late miscarriage and preterm birth (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23–0.79; PMID: 30792154). A systematic review and meta-analysis further suggests that continuing metformin through the first trimester may reduce miscarriage risk compared to placebo or stopping metformin after conception (PMID: 40473092), while the one RCT showing no difference in miscarriage rates focused on early miscarriage as a secondary outcome and found metformin improved live-birth rates overall (PMID: 22419702).

**Key findings:**
- In a prospective pilot study, first-trimester spontaneous abortion occurred in only 10% of PCOS women receiving metformin throughout pregnancy compared to 73% in their own prior pregnancies without metformin (PMID: 11163815).
- A prospective cohort study found early pregnancy loss rates of 11.6% in the metformin group versus 36.3% in controls who discontinued metformin (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.11–0.42; PMID: 17162710).
- Continuing metformin until 12 weeks gestation reduced miscarriage from 32% to 4% in PCOS patients, with statistically significant differences versus stopping at conception (PMID: 20441061).
- A pooled individual participant data analysis of three RCTs found late miscarriage or preterm birth occurred in 5% of metformin-treated women versus 10% of placebo-treated women (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23–0.79, p=0.004; PMID: 30792154).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 RCTs found that preconception metformin continued through the first trimester was associated with a possible reduction in miscarriage (OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.32–1.25) and that indirect comparisons favored continuation over stopping metformin after a positive pregnancy test (miscarriage OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.17–1.16; PMID: 40473092).
- A multicenter double-blind RCT found miscarriage rates were low and similar between metformin (15.2%) and placebo (17.9%) groups, though metformin significantly improved pregnancy and live-birth rates (PMID: 22419702).
- An observational study in previously infertile PCOS women found metformin was associated with a lower combined risk of miscarriage or preterm delivery (13.3% vs. 24.4%, p=0.046; OR 0.417, p=0.040 on adjusted analysis; PMID: 39030462).

### Preeclampsia

#### Literature — Preeclampsia

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 12

The evidence on metformin for preeclampsia is mixed across study designs and comparators. Meta-analyses and RCTs comparing metformin to insulin show a reduced risk of preeclampsia (PMID: 29044702, 33927270, 38124287), while RCTs and large cohort studies comparing metformin to placebo show no significant benefit and in some cases numerically higher rates (PMID: 20926533, 39236318, 39334302, 42827236). The one RCT directly targeting preterm preeclampsia showed a non-significant trend toward prolonged gestation as its primary outcome, with benefit only in per-protocol subgroups (PMID: 34551918).

**Key findings:**
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs comparing metformin vs. insulin found a reduced risk of preeclampsia (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.48–0.95), but no significant difference vs. placebo/control in 5 RCTs (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.33–2.26) (PMID: 29044702).
- A meta-analysis of 35 RCTs found metformin associated with lower odds of preeclampsia vs. other treatments (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.50–0.95), though heterogeneity was substantial (I2=55%) (PMID: 33927270).
- A meta-analysis of 24 RCTs in GDM found metformin significantly reduced preeclampsia risk compared to insulin (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.48–0.78) (PMID: 38124287).
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in preterm preeclampsia found a non-significant prolongation of gestation (median difference 7.6 days, GMR 1.39, 95% CI 0.99–1.95; P=0.057) as the primary outcome, with significant benefit only in per-protocol subgroups; composite maternal and neonatal outcomes did not differ (PMID: 34551918).
- A secondary analysis of the MOMPOD RCT found metformin was not associated with lower odds of preterm preeclampsia before 37 weeks (aOR 1.04, 95% CI 0.70–1.56) or before 34 weeks (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 0.73–2.81) compared to placebo in women with early gestational or type 2 diabetes (PMID: 39236318).
- A two-country cohort analysis (Scotland and Sweden, n>11,000) found metformin not associated with altered risk of preeclampsia (pooled RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.73–1.28) or any hypertensive disorder of pregnancy (pooled RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.79–1.18) (PMID: 39334302).
- A randomized placebo-controlled multicenter trial in PCOS found preeclampsia prevalence numerically higher in the metformin group (7.4%) vs. placebo (3.7%), though not statistically significant (P=0.18) (PMID: 20926533).
- A network meta-analysis of RCTs found evidence for metformin in preventing preeclampsia to be inconsistent with wide confidence intervals, while higher-dose vitamin D showed stronger associations with preeclampsia reduction (PMID: 42827236).
- A meta-analysis of 3 RCTs in obese pregnant women without diabetes found metformin did not significantly reduce preeclampsia risk (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.10–1.68; p=0.22; I2=81%), with high heterogeneity precluding firm conclusions (PMID: 42924392).
- A systematic review found metformin significantly reduced severe preeclampsia incidence (12.1% vs. 20.7%, aOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.18–0.81) and reduced maternal sEng and sFlt-1 levels, but was based on a small number of studies with clinical diversity (PMID: 40852348).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis found metformin had greater preventive effects for pregnancy-induced hypertension than for preeclampsia specifically, and concluded dosage, gestational age, and treatment timing require further evaluation (PMID: 30399639).
- An RCT in GDM in a developing country found preeclampsia was significantly less common in metformin-treated groups compared to insulin alone, though the study was open-label (PMID: 25467617).

The evidence on metformin for PCOS is mixed, with clear benefits in some domains (menstrual regulation, hyperandrogenism, insulin sensitivity, and OHSS reduction in IVF) but inferiority to clomiphene for live birth in infertility treatment and no consistent improvement in overall IVF outcomes. A large RCT found metformin's live-birth rate (7.2%) was significantly lower than clomiphene (22.5%) for infertile PCOS women (PMID: 17287476), while controlled trials and meta-analyses support metformin's efficacy in reducing testosterone and improving menstrual regularity (PMID: 10634377, PMID: 36763111). The net picture depends heavily on the clinical endpoint being targeted, with metabolic and hormonal benefits reasonably established but reproductive outcomes showing inconsistent or inferior results compared to alternatives.

**Key findings:**
- A large RCT of 626 women found metformin's live-birth rate (7.2%) was significantly inferior to clomiphene (22.5%) and combination therapy (26.8%) for infertile PCOS women (PMID: 17287476).
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled 6-month trial found metformin improved menstrual frequency, reduced free testosterone, and increased insulin sensitivity in PCOS women, with 54.8% of subjects showing striking improvements in menstrual abnormalities in the open long-term phase (PMID: 10634377).
- An uncontrolled study reported that 39 of 43 (91%) previously amenorrheic PCOS women resumed normal menses on metformin, with improvements independent of weight loss (PMID: 10206447).
- A meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials found metformin significantly reduced total testosterone levels [SMD: -0.46 (95% CI: -0.89 to -0.02)] and free androgen index in PCOS patients (PMID: 36763111).
- A randomized placebo-controlled IVF trial found metformin significantly improved clinical pregnancy rates (38.5% vs. 16.3%, P=0.023) and reduced severe OHSS incidence (3.8% vs. 20.4%, P=0.023), though it did not improve fertilization rates or oocyte yield (PMID: 16501038).
- A prospective randomized IVF trial concluded that metformin does not lead to any improvement in IVF/ICSI outcomes among PCOS patients (PMID: 16169430).
- A randomized controlled trial in PCOS patients with reduced ovarian reserve found metformin worsened gonadotropin response, with significantly fewer retrieved oocytes and higher cancellation rates, leading to early trial termination (PMID: 21917254).
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial in clomiphene-resistant PCOS women found no significant difference between metformin and placebo in spontaneous menstruation, ovulation induction, or pregnancy rates (PMID: 11994052).
- A randomized double-blind crossover trial in obese PCOS women found that high-dose metformin (2000 mg/day) did not significantly improve ovulation rates, serum sex steroids, glucose disposal, or other metabolic markers (PMID: 19692630).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis found metformin was inferior to combined oral contraceptives (COCPs) on free androgen index, SHBG, and testosterone, but superior for insulin and triglyceride reduction; combination therapy showed additional benefits over COCP alone for insulin resistance (PMID: 37554096).
- A systematic review of RCTs in adolescent PCOS found that while COCs better controlled hyperandrogenic symptoms, insulin-sensitizing regimens including metformin showed superior outcomes in ovulation rates, insulin sensitivity, and body composition, with combination therapy offering balanced reproductive and metabolic benefits (PMID: 40491634).
- A randomized controlled IVF trial in clomiphene-resistant PCOS women found metformin improved embryo quality, implantation rate, and significantly reduced OHSS and abortion rates, but did not significantly increase overall pregnancy rates compared to placebo (PMID: 19757275).

### Insulin Resistance

#### Literature — Insulin Resistance

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The evidence on metformin for insulin resistance is mixed across populations and study designs. In obese insulin-resistant adolescents, two randomized controlled trials showed significant improvements in fasting insulin and insulin sensitivity indices with metformin versus placebo (PMID: 18556965, PMID: 16595599), while a third RCT found similar HOMA-IR improvements in both metformin and placebo groups (PMID: 20639355). In adults with type 1 diabetes, a 26-week randomized placebo-controlled trial found no significant effect of metformin on hepatic insulin resistance (PMID: 41285865), and in women with PCOS-like syndrome, metformin monotherapy produced the smallest improvement in menstrual regularity compared to other interventions, though HOMA-IR did improve (PMID: 42517336).

**Key findings:**
- In a 6-month randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial of obese adolescents with hyperinsulinemia, metformin significantly reduced fasting insulin (19.2 to 11.1 µU/ml, p<0.001) and HOMA-IR (4.95 to 2.6, p<0.001) compared to placebo (PMID: 18556965).
- In a randomized double-blind crossover trial of obese insulin-resistant children and adolescents, metformin had a significantly greater treatment effect over placebo for fasting insulin (-2.2 mU/liter, P=0.011) and body composition measures (PMID: 16595599).
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of obese insulin-resistant adolescents, HOMA-IR improved similarly in both the metformin and placebo groups (decreased 54% vs 73% respectively), with most differences not reaching statistical significance, suggesting the improvement may be attributable to improved lifestyle compliance rather than metformin specifically (PMID: 20639355).
- In a 26-week randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in adults with type 1 diabetes, metformin 1500 mg showed no significant difference from placebo in reducing hepatic endogenous glucose production (mean difference 0.2 µmol/kgFFM/min, p=0.53) (PMID: 41285865).
- In a 12-week RCT in women with PCOS-like syndrome, metformin monotherapy produced the lowest rate of menstrual cycle regularization (39.6%) compared to dietary restriction (72.9%), inositol (64.6%), and combination therapy (85.4%), though all groups including metformin showed improvement in HOMA-IR (PMID: 42517336).
- Combination therapy with metformin, inositol, and dietary restriction produced the greatest reduction in HOMA-IR (-2.64, 95% CI -2.82 to -2.46, p<0.001) compared to any single-modality intervention including metformin alone (PMID: 42517336).

### Infertility

#### Literature — Infertility

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 15

The evidence on metformin for infertility is mixed, with comparably credible controlled trials pointing in both directions. Several randomized controlled trials and a multicenter double-blind placebo-controlled trial show metformin significantly improves pregnancy and live-birth rates in PCOS women (PMIDs 22419702, 21606131, 18022165), while other well-powered RCTs demonstrate that metformin is inferior to clomiphene as monotherapy for live birth (PMID 17287476), worsens ovarian response in PCOS women with reduced ovarian reserve (PMID 21917254), and does not significantly improve pregnancy rates compared to placebo or active comparators in certain populations (PMIDs 11994052, 30847561, 41618368, 38694345). The net picture across direct clinical outcome data from controlled trials is genuinely bidirectional with no clear overall superiority.

**Key findings:**
- In a large multicenter double-blind placebo-controlled RCT of 320 PCOS women, metformin significantly improved pregnancy rate (53.6% vs 40.4%) and live-birth rate (41.9% vs 28.8%) compared to placebo (PMID 22419702).
- In a multicenter double-blind RCT of 150 non-obese PCOS women, intention-to-treat analysis showed significantly higher overall clinical pregnancy rate (50.0% vs 33.3%, P=0.0391) and live-birth rate (48.6% vs 32.0%, P=0.0383) with metformin versus placebo, though there was no significant effect on ART outcomes per se (PMID 21606131).
- In a large RCT of 626 infertile PCOS women, the live-birth rate with metformin monotherapy was only 7.2% versus 22.5% with clomiphene (P<0.001), establishing clomiphene as superior to metformin for achieving live birth (PMID 17287476).
- A prospective randomized double-blind placebo-controlled RCT in PCOS women with reduced ovarian reserve was stopped early because metformin significantly worsened ovarian response, reducing retrieved oocytes and metaphase II oocytes compared to placebo (PMID 21917254).
- A randomized placebo-controlled double-blind crossover study found no significant difference between metformin and placebo in ovulation induction or pregnancy rates in clomiphene-resistant PCOS women (PMID 11994052).
- A prospective randomized study found that metformin pretreatment significantly improved clinical pregnancy rate (38.2% vs 16.7%) and implantation rate (15.3% vs 6.2%) in IVM cycles for clomiphene-resistant PCOS women (PMID 18022165).

### Heart Failure

#### Literature — Heart Failure

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 8

The evidence on metformin for heart failure is mixed overall. Several small RCTs and meta-analyses report benefits on surrogate endpoints such as myocardial efficiency, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, VE/VCO2 slope, NT-proBNP, and mortality risk in diabetic CHF patients (PMIDs: 31863557, 37816240, 22740509, 39277567, 31362743, 18429758), yet a systematic review of RCTs found no consistent effect on left ventricular function and inconclusive results across diabetic and nondiabetic populations (PMID: 32157481), and a placebo-controlled RCT found no improvement in cardiac function despite metabolic benefits (PMID: 39829685). Hard clinical endpoints such as HF hospitalization remain non-significant in meta-analyses (PMID: 39277567), and the primary exercise endpoint (peak VO2) was not improved in a controlled trial (PMID: 22740509), leaving the net clinical benefit uncertain.

**Key findings:**
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in non-diabetic insulin-resistant HFrEF patients found metformin improved myocardial efficiency (WMI) by ~20% and reduced myocardial oxygen consumption, though resting/exercise EF and exercise capacity did not differ between groups (PMID: 31863557).
- A randomized controlled trial in nondiabetic HFrEF patients found metformin improved total antioxidant capacity and preserved left ventricular mass index compared to standard of care, but changes in LVEF and MDA were not significantly different between groups (PMID: 37816240).
- A double-blind placebo-controlled trial in non-diabetic insulin-resistant CHF patients found metformin significantly reduced insulin resistance and improved the VE/VCO2 slope, but did not improve the primary endpoint of peak VO2 (PMID: 22740509).
- A meta-analysis of 15 RCTs in T2DM+CHF patients found metformin significantly reduced all-cause mortality (RR=0.72) and cardiovascular mortality (RR=0.52) and improved NT-proBNP, but did not significantly reduce HF hospitalization risk (RR=0.85) or improve LVEF or LVEDD (PMID: 39277567).
- A systematic review of RCTs found that while metformin improved myocardial oxygen consumption and reduced NT-proBNP and LDL inconsistently, it did not affect left ventricular function overall, and additional RCTs were deemed necessary (PMID: 32157481).
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in HF patients with type 2 diabetes found metformin improved glycemic control and postprandial GLP-1 secretion but did not influence cardiac function or TMAO levels (PMID: 39829685).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 40 studies in CAD patients found metformin reduced cardiovascular mortality (aHR=0.81), all-cause mortality (aHR=0.67), and CV events (aHR=0.83), with subgroup analysis showing reduced all-cause mortality in HF patients (aHR=0.84) and reduced CV events in HF patients (aHR=0.83) (PMID: 31362743).
- An uncontrolled cohort study in CHF+T2DM patients found metformin use was associated with improved NYHA functional class, central hemodynamics, quality of life, fewer CHF decompensations, and reduced sympathoadrenal activation, with no cases of lactic acidosis (PMID: 18429758).

### Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

#### Literature — Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 12

The evidence on metformin as adjunct therapy to insulin in type 1 diabetes is mixed, with no consistent signal on the primary clinical outcome of glycemic control (HbA1c). Several smaller randomized controlled trials and one meta-analysis report HbA1c reductions with metformin (PMIDs 12502670, 16978371, 14514347, 35249270, 35351535, 37599482, 40512873), while larger or longer-duration RCTs and one network meta-analysis find no significant HbA1c benefit (PMIDs 26624824, 18852875, 19496776, 41044631). Across studies, metformin consistently reduces insulin dose and body weight, but at the cost of increased gastrointestinal adverse events, and the glycemic benefit—the primary therapeutic target—remains unproven in the most rigorous evidence.

**Key findings:**
- A large multicenter double-blind RCT (n=140 overweight adolescents) found no significant difference in HbA1c at 26 weeks between metformin and placebo (mean difference 0%, P=0.92), though metformin reduced insulin dose and BMI z-score (PMID 26624824).
- A 12-month double-blind RCT (n=100 adults with poorly controlled T1DM) found no improvement in HbA1c with metformin vs. placebo (difference 0.13%, P=0.422), though insulin dose and body weight were reduced (PMID 18852875).
- A 24-week double-blind RCT (n=24 overweight T1DM patients) found no significant difference in HbA1c between metformin and placebo (P=0.26), but metformin significantly reduced total daily insulin dose and body weight (PMID 19496776).
- A network meta-analysis of adolescent T1DM RCTs (n=764) found that no metformin dose regimen significantly lowered HbA1c versus placebo, though metformin 2.0 g/day reduced BMI, LDL-C, and insulin requirements (PMID 41044631).
- A 3-month double-blind RCT (n=27 adolescents) found HbA1c was 0.6% lower in the metformin group than placebo (P<0.05), with reduced insulin dosage and improved fasting glucose (PMID 12502670).
- A 16-week crossover RCT (n=15 overweight adults) found HbA1c significantly lower after metformin (7.8%) vs. placebo (8.6%, P<0.005), with reduced fasting plasma glucose and total daily insulin dose (PMID 16978371).
- A 3-month double-blind RCT (n=26 adolescents) found HbA1c decreased significantly in the metformin group (9.6% to 8.7%, P<0.05) but was unchanged in the placebo group, with improved peripheral insulin sensitivity (PMID 14514347).
- A 9-month double-blind RCT (n=50 adolescents) found HbA1c and insulin dosage were significantly lower in the metformin group than placebo (P<0.001 and P=0.04, respectively), with improved lipid profile (PMID 35249270).
- A 24-week RCT (n=80 adolescents with T1DM and microvascular complications) found metformin significantly decreased HbA1c, CRP, UACR, total cholesterol, and CIMT compared with placebo (P<0.001) (PMID 35351535).
- A 3-month clinical trial (n=80 Pakistani adolescents) found a significant reduction in HbA1c (P<0.001) and random blood glucose (P=0.006) in the metformin group compared with insulin-only group (PMID 37599482).
- An updated meta-analysis of 29 RCTs (n=2051) found metformin significantly reduced HbA1c in adolescents at 3 months (MD=-0.45, 95% CI:-0.79,-0.11) and in adults at 6 months (MD=-0.70, 95% CI:-1.10,-0.30), but no statistical difference was identified at other follow-up endpoints (PMID 40512873).
- A randomized placebo-controlled multicentre study in 126 pregnant women with T1DM found no significant difference in total insulin requirement or glycemic control, but metformin reduced prandial insulin increase and improved weight gain control (PMID 40916351).
- Gastrointestinal adverse events were significantly more frequent with metformin than placebo in both adolescents (RR=1.74) and adults (RR=3.24) across RCTs (PMID 40512873), and also reported in individual trials (PMID 26624824).
- Increased frequency of mild hypoglycemia was observed with metformin in some RCTs (PMID 12502670, PMID 19496776).

### Sepsis

#### Literature — Sepsis

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 8

The preponderance of evidence from multiple systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and observational studies supports that metformin use (both preadmission and during hospitalization) is associated with reduced mortality in diabetic patients with sepsis. Four meta-analyses consistently report significant mortality reductions (ORs ranging from 0.57 to 0.74) associated with metformin exposure (PMIDs: 30777119, 32166255, 35002982, 42087873), and two additional observational studies corroborate these findings for in-hospital and post-septic shock administration (PMIDs: 35120041, 40394230). The single contradicting study (PMID: 32146543) is a single cohort study that found no significant association with 30-day mortality, but it is outweighed by the consistent positive signal across multiple higher-level systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

**Key findings:**
- A meta-analysis of 5 cohort studies (1,282 patients) found preadmission metformin use was associated with significantly lower mortality in septic diabetic patients (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.43–0.79, P=0.001) (PMID: 30777119).
- A meta-analysis of 8 observational studies (4,144 patients) found premorbid metformin exposure was associated with reduced mortality in sepsis (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.80) without significant association with hyperlactatemia (PMID: 32166255).
- A meta-analysis of 11 studies (8,195 patients) found preadmission metformin use was associated with lower mortality (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62–0.88, P<0.01) without significant increases in serum creatinine or lactic acid (PMID: 35002982).
- The most recent and largest meta-analysis of 14 studies (12,687 patients) found preadmission metformin use was associated with reduced overall mortality (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.75, P<0.00001), with significant reductions in 28-day, 90-day, 365-day, and in-hospital mortality, though 30-day, 60-day, and ICU mortality did not reach significance (PMID: 42087873).
- A retrospective cohort study of 14,847 ICU patients found metformin exposure during hospitalization was associated with decreased 90-day mortality (11.9% vs. 22.7%; OR 0.46, 95% CI 0.35–0.60) and reduced severe acute kidney injury in propensity score-matched diabetic sepsis patients (PMID: 35120041).
- A retrospective observational study of 320 septic shock patients with type 2 diabetes found metformin administration within 48 hours was independently associated with decreased 90-day mortality (aHR 0.371, 95% CI 0.153–0.900, P=0.028) and 365-day mortality (aHR 0.453, 95% CI 0.219–0.937, P=0.033) (PMID: 40394230).
- A nationwide cohort study of 77,337 diabetic patients found that prior metformin therapy was not significantly associated with 30-day mortality after sepsis diagnosis (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.75–1.17, P=0.571) after propensity score adjustment (PMID: 32146543).

### Hyperandrogenism

#### Literature — Hyperandrogenism

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 10

The preponderance of evidence from randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses supports metformin's efficacy in reducing biochemical hyperandrogenism, particularly total testosterone and free androgen index. Two systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs demonstrate significant reductions in total testosterone and FAI with metformin versus placebo (PMIDs 36763111, 35434762), and multiple individual RCTs confirm reductions in serum androgens in PCOS and T1D populations (PMIDs 16520442, 25304843, 23845740, 24280743). The two contradicting studies (PMIDs 7608255, 18410553) are small, short-duration trials with design limitations—one showing diet alone was sufficient and metformin added no benefit, and one showing rimonabant superior to metformin—which do not offset the consistent positive signal from higher-quality and larger bodies of evidence.

**Key findings:**
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 17–18 RCTs found metformin significantly reduced total testosterone [SMD: -0.46 (95% CI: -0.89 to -0.02)] and regulated FAI values compared to placebo in PCOS patients (PMID 36763111).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 92 RCTs found significant reductions in total testosterone with metformin versus placebo [SMD: -0.33; 95% CI -0.49 to -0.17, p<0.0001, moderate grade evidence] in women with PCOS (PMID 35434762).
- A randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial in 22 adolescents with PCOS showed metformin significantly lowered mean serum testosterone (-38.3 ng/dL vs -0.86 ng/dL with placebo) and increased the relative risk of menses 2.50-fold (PMID 16520442).
- A four-day randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial demonstrated metformin reduced LH-stimulated testosterone (p=0.03) and borderline reduced FAI (p=0.05) within 2 days, with a sustained decline in serum testosterone at 3 months independent of weight or insulin sensitivity changes (PMID 25304843).
- A 6-month RCT in 56 PCOS patients found metformin alone (1700 mg/day) significantly reduced circulating testosterone, androstenedione, and hirsutism score, and restored regular menses in ~82% of patients (PMID 23845740).
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 24 hyperandrogenic adolescents with type 1 diabetes found metformin significantly decreased testosterone, free androgen index, androstenedione, and 17-OH progesterone compared to placebo, though clinical parameters such as hirsutism and ovulation did not significantly differ (PMID 24280743).
- A meta-analysis of 36 RCTs found metformin was inferior to combined oral contraceptive pills (COCP) on FAI, SHBG, and testosterone, but metformin was superior for insulin and triglyceride reduction, suggesting complementary rather than absent efficacy (PMID 37554096).
- An open randomized crossover study in 32 post-menarcheal girls with preclinical androgen excess found that metformin alone improved endocrine-metabolic abnormalities, with flutamide addition providing further benefit only in girls with shorter androgen receptor CAG repeats (PMID 17984247).
- A double-blind RCT in 24 obese hirsute women on a low-calorie diet found that metformin added no significant benefit over diet alone in reducing androgens or insulin, with both groups improving similarly (PMID 7608255).
- A randomized open-label parallel study in 20 obese PCOS women found rimonabant significantly reduced testosterone and FAI while metformin showed no significant change in these parameters over 12 weeks (PMID 18410553).

### Atherosclerosis

#### Literature — Atherosclerosis

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The evidence is mixed, with some studies supporting metformin's benefit on atherosclerosis-related outcomes and others failing to show a significant effect. A crossover controlled trial found metformin significantly improved peripheral arterial blood flow in patients with peripheral atherosclerosis (PMID: 6209500), and a network meta-analysis found metformin significantly reduced cIMT progression compared to placebo (PMID: 38883606). However, two randomized placebo-controlled trials—one in type 2 diabetes (PMID: 26916684) and one in type 1 diabetes (PMID: 28615149)—found no significant reduction in mean carotid IMT with metformin, leaving the net clinical picture uncertain.

**Key findings:**
- In a crossover controlled trial of 15 patients with peripheral atherosclerosis, metformin 850 mg three times daily significantly increased arterial blood flow by 17.3% at 3 months and 40.0% at 6 months compared to placebo, with the effect reversible upon switching to placebo (PMID: 6209500).
- A network meta-analysis of 25 clinical trials (6675 patients) found metformin significantly reduced cIMT progression compared to placebo (MD = -0.05 mm, 95% CI -0.09 to -0.02), ranking it among the more effective antidiabetic drugs for this outcome (PMID: 38883606).
- A randomized placebo-controlled trial in 412 patients with type 2 diabetes found no significant difference in carotid IMT change between metformin and placebo after 18 months (between-group difference 0.012 mm, 95% CI -0.003 to 0.026, p=0.11), though the trial was underpowered at 46% of planned sample size (PMID: 26916684).
- The REMOVAL trial, a double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 428 adults with type 1 diabetes, found that metformin did not significantly reduce progression of mean cIMT (-0.005 mm per year, 95% CI -0.012 to 0.002; p=0.1664), although maximal cIMT was significantly reduced as a tertiary outcome (PMID: 28615149).

### Medulloblastoma

#### Literature — Medulloblastoma

**Evidence strength:** weak, supports
**Relevant studies:** 1

A single preclinical study (PMID: 36278239) investigated metformin in Sonic hedgehog (Shh) subgroup medulloblastoma using cell lines and a xenograft mouse model, finding that metformin inhibited proliferation, metastasis, and tumor growth in vivo. The evidence supports repurposing metformin for medulloblastoma, though it is limited entirely to non-human (in vitro and animal) models. No clinical data in humans are available from the retrieved literature.

**Key findings:**
- Metformin inhibited proliferation and metastasis of the Shh subgroup medulloblastoma cell line (Daoy), with the anti-proliferative effect associated with apoptosis induction and G0/G1 cell cycle arrest (PMID: 36278239).
- In a xenograft murine model, metformin inhibited medulloblastoma tumor growth in vivo (PMID: 36278239).
- Metformin decreased mRNA and protein expression levels of the Shh signaling pathway, and this effect was reversed by AMPK siRNA, indicating an AMPK-mediated mechanism (PMID: 36278239).
- Pro-apoptotic and cell cycle arrest effects of metformin on Daoy cells were reversed by Shh pathway activators, confirming that metformin's effects are mediated through inhibition of the Shh pathway (PMID: 36278239).

### Prostate Cancer

#### Literature — Prostate Cancer

**Evidence strength:** strong, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 11

The highest-quality clinical evidence consistently fails to show that metformin improves prostate cancer outcomes. The phase III MAST trial found no reduction in progression for low-risk prostate cancer on active surveillance (HR 1.09, p=0.59) and even identified harm in obese patients (PMID: 41166665), while the large phase III STAMPEDE trial found no overall survival benefit when metformin was added to standard of care for metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (HR 0.91, p=0.15) (PMID: 40639383). Supporting signals from observational and smaller studies are insufficient to offset this consistent null or negative finding from well-powered randomized controlled trials.

**Key findings:**
- Phase III MAST RCT (n=408): metformin did not reduce progression-free survival vs placebo in low-risk prostate cancer on active surveillance (HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.79–1.52, p=0.59), and was associated with increased pathologic progression in obese patients (HR 2.36, p=0.0092) (PMID: 41166665).
- Phase III STAMPEDE RCT (n=1874): adding metformin to standard of care did not improve overall survival in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.80–1.03, p=0.15) (PMID: 40639383).
- A randomized placebo-controlled preprostatectomy trial found no differences in tissue or serum biomarkers of drug activity between metformin and placebo arms despite confirmed prostate tissue distribution of metformin (PMID: 28692586).
- In the REDUCE study, metformin use was not significantly associated with total, low-grade, or high-grade prostate cancer diagnosis in diabetic men undergoing protocol-dictated biopsies (OR 1.19, p=0.50 for total PCa) (PMID: 26353947).
- A phase 2 RCT (BIMET-1) found no improvement in rates of undetectable PSA at 32 weeks with metformin plus bicalutamide vs bicalutamide alone, and was stopped early for predicted inability to achieve its primary endpoint (PMID: 35079115).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis including STAMPEDE confirmed no oncological benefit in unselected patients; a high-volume subgroup survival signal (HR 0.79, p=0.007) was hypothesis-generating only, derived from a single trial with a non-significant volume interaction (p=0.12) (PMID: 42875601).
- A meta-analysis of real-world studies found metformin associated with lower PCa incidence (RR 0.82) but not with reduced recurrence (RR 0.97, not significant) or mortality (RR 0.94, not significant) (PMID: 39014063).
- A large national population-based retrospective cohort study reported an inverse association between metformin duration and prostate cancer risk (Q4 HR 0.571), but this observational finding is outweighed by null results from controlled trials (PMID: 36974927).
- A target trial emulation study found metformin associated with lower PSA progression (HR 0.71) and CRPC progression (HR 0.67), but authors noted these findings were exploratory due to limited events and require confirmation in randomized studies (PMID: 43046007).
- Metformin added to ADT consistently reduced metabolic markers (waist circumference, HbA1c, fasting glucose) across RCTs, but this metabolic benefit did not translate into demonstrated oncological benefit in unselected patients (PMID: 42875601).

### Breast Cancer

#### Literature — Breast Cancer

**Evidence strength:** strong, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 11

The preponderance of high-quality clinical evidence contradicts the repurposing of metformin for breast cancer treatment in non-diabetic patients. The largest and most definitive evidence comes from the MA.32 phase III randomized controlled trial (n=3,649), which found no improvement in invasive disease-free survival or overall survival with metformin versus placebo (PMID: 35608580), and a secondary analysis of the same trial showed no reduction in new primary cancer risk (PMID: 37695982). A meta-analysis of RCTs found no significant improvement in pathological complete response rates with metformin in the neoadjuvant setting (PMID: 41161296), and a phase II RCT in metastatic breast cancer showed no benefit in progression-free or overall survival (PMID: 31472446); while some biomarker and small RCT data show biological activity, these do not offset the consistent null findings from controlled clinical outcome trials.

**Key findings:**
- In the MA.32 phase III RCT (n=3,649), metformin versus placebo did not significantly improve invasive disease-free survival (HR 1.01; 95% CI, 0.84-1.21; P=.93) or overall survival (HR 1.10; 95% CI, 0.86-1.41; P=.47) in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer patients without diabetes (PMID: 35608580).
- A secondary analysis of MA.32 found that metformin did not reduce the risk of new primary cancer development; 102 patients in the metformin arm versus 82 in the placebo arm developed new invasive cancers (HR 1.25; 95% CI, 0.94-1.68; P=.13) (PMID: 37695982).
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found no statistically significant difference in pathological complete response rates between metformin and control groups in the neoadjuvant breast cancer setting (RR=1.21; 95% CI: 0.85-1.71; P=0.28) (PMID: 41161296).
- A phase II double-blind RCT in metastatic breast cancer found no significant benefit of metformin added to chemotherapy on progression-free survival (mean PFS 5.4 vs 6.3 months; HR 1.2, 95% CI 0.63-2.31) or overall survival (mean OS 20.2 vs 24.2 months; HR 1.68, 95% CI 0.79-3.55) (PMID: 31472446).
- A phase 2 open-label RCT (McBETH, n=242) evaluating metformin added to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in HER2-positive and triple-negative breast cancer found no significant improvement in pathological complete response rates (43.7% vs 41.7%; p=0.75) (PMID: 41348549).
- A meta-analysis of 15 studies found no statistically significant reduction in breast cancer risk among metformin users with type 2 diabetes compared to non-metformin users (summary RR 0.82; 95% CI, 0.60-1.12) (PMID: 36533539).
- A presurgical randomized trial found no significant modulation of apoptosis (TUNEL) by metformin overall versus placebo in non-diabetic breast cancer patients (P=0.2), though a non-significant trend differed by insulin resistance status (PMID: 24157825).
- A preoperative window-of-opportunity randomized trial found metformin produced favorable biomarker changes (upregulation of pAMPK, downregulation of pAkt, reduction in Ki67) in primary breast cancer, suggesting biological activity but only at the biomarker level (PMID: 25682077).
- A presurgical randomized trial found metformin did not affect Ki-67 proliferation in DCIS overall, though a subgroup analysis suggested lower Ki-67 in HER2-positive/ER-positive DCIS with metformin (12% vs 32%; P=.004), a finding requiring further investigation (PMID: 26276754).
- A small RCT (METNEO, n=70) found metformin added to neoadjuvant AC-T chemotherapy significantly improved pathological complete response rates and clinical response, with upregulation of death receptors DR4 and DR5 and reduction of CD133+ cancer stem cells (PMID: 39113190).
- A scoping review of 40 RCTs noted that most studies did not present results by breast cancer phenotype, and that limited subgroup evidence suggested potential benefit in HER2+ patients, but overall findings were inconclusive and require further investigation (PMID: 40841629).

### Endothelial Dysfunction

#### Literature — Endothelial Dysfunction

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 8

The preponderance of controlled trial evidence supports metformin's benefit for endothelial function. Multiple randomized placebo-controlled trials in type 2 diabetes (PMID: 11300445), metabolic syndrome (PMID: 16115299), hypertension (PMID: 23140261), first-degree relatives of diabetic patients (PMID: 16644641), and children with type 1 diabetes (PMID: 29040598) consistently demonstrate significant improvements in endothelium-dependent vasodilation with metformin versus placebo. The mixed verdicts from a long-term RCT (PMID: 23981104) and a PCOS trial (PMID: 31806099) reflect partial or subgroup-dependent effects rather than contradictory evidence, and a scoping review (PMID: 41057192) confirms a trend toward improvement across biomarkers while noting methodological gaps.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of 65 patients with metabolic syndrome, metformin 500 mg twice daily for 3 months significantly improved flow-mediated dilatation (FMD) from 7.4% to 12.4% vs. no change with placebo (P=0.0016) (PMID: 16115299).
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of 44 patients with type 2 diabetes, metformin 500 mg twice daily for 12 weeks significantly improved acetylcholine-stimulated forearm blood flow (P=0.0027) with no effect on endothelium-independent responses (PMID: 11300445).
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of 62 patients with primary hypertension, a single dose of metformin 500 mg prevented the decline in brachial artery FMD caused by an acute glucose load and inhibited the glucose-load-induced decline in antioxidant markers (PMID: 23140261).
- In a double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial of 31 first-degree relatives of type 2 diabetic patients with metabolic syndrome and normal glucose tolerance, metformin improved endothelium-dependent forearm blood flow responses without affecting endothelium-independent responses (PMID: 16644641).
- In a 12-month double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial of 90 children with type 1 diabetes, metformin improved glyceryl trinitrate-mediated dilatation (vascular smooth muscle function) by 3.3 percentage units (95% CI 0.3–6.3, P=0.03) independent of HbA1c (PMID: 29040598).
- In a 4.3-year randomized placebo-controlled trial of 390 insulin-treated type 2 diabetes patients, metformin significantly reduced vWF, sVCAM-1, t-PA, PAI-1, CRP, and sICAM-1, but had no effect on urinary albumin excretion or sE-selectin, yielding a mixed result across endothelial biomarkers (PMID: 23981104).
- In a randomized trial of 42 women with PCOS, metformin did not improve endothelial function overall, but did significantly improve RH-PAT in the subgroup with abnormal baseline endothelial function (1.3±0.3 vs. 1.7±0.3; P<0.001), while showing no effect in those with normal baseline function (PMID: 31806099).
- A scoping review of 56 studies (4620 participants) found metformin showed a trend toward improvement for 7 of 19 endothelial biomarkers in non-diabetic conditions, with studies lacking an active comparator reporting significant results favouring metformin in 83.3% of cases versus 20% in active-comparator studies (PMID: 41057192).

WARNING Metformin Hydrochloride Lactic acidosis: Lactic acidosis is a rare, but serious, metabolic complication that can occur due to metformin accumulation during treatment with Glipizide and Metformin HCl Tablets; when it occurs, it is fatal in approximately 50% of cases. Lactic acidosis may also occur in association with a number of pathophysiologic conditions, including diabetes mellitus, and whenever there is significant tissue hypoperfusion and hypoxemia. Lactic acidosis is characterized by elevated blood lactate levels (>5 mmol/L), decreased blood pH, electrolyte disturbances with an increased anion gap, and an increased lactate/pyruvate ratio. When metformin is implicated as the cause of lactic acidosis, metformin plasma levels >5 μg/mL are generally found. The reported incidence of lactic acidosis in patients receiving Metformin hydrochloride is very low (approximately 0.03 cases/1000 patient-years, with approximately 0.015 fatal cases/1000 patient-years). In more than 20,000 patient-years exposure to metformin in clinical trials, there were no reports of lactic acidosis. Reported cases have occurred primarily in diabetic patients with significant renal insufficiency, including both intrinsic renal disease and renal hypoperfusion, often in the setting of multiple concomitant medical/surgical problems and multiple concomitant medications. Patients with congestive heart failure requiring pharmacologic management, in particular those with unstable or acute congestive heart failure who are at risk of hypoperfusion and hypoxemia, are at increased risk of lactic acidosis. The risk of lactic acidosis increases with the degree of renal dysfunction and the patient's age. The risk of lactic acidosis may, therefore, be significantly decreased by regular monitoring of renal function in patients taking metformin and by use of the minimum effective dose of metformin. In particular, treatment of the elderly should be accompanied by careful monitoring of renal function. Glipizide and Metformin HCl Tablets treatment should not be initiated in patients ≥80 years of age unless measurement of creatinine clearance demonstrates that renal function is not reduced, as these patients are more susceptible to developing lactic acidosis. In addition, Glipizide and Metformin HCl Tablets should be promptly withheld in the presence of any condition associated with hypoxemia, dehydration, or sepsis. Because impaired hepatic function may significantly limit the ability to clear lactate, Glipizide and Metformin HCl Tablets should generally be avoided in patients with clinical or laboratory evidence of hepatic disease. Patients should be cautioned against excessive alcohol intake, either acute or chronic, when taking Glipizide and Metformin HCl Tablets, since alcohol potentiates the effects of metformin hydrochloride on lactate metabolism.
