## Drug Safety

_Drug-wide safety signal — applies to the drug generally, not to any single candidate indication below._

**Regulatory label:** FDA label boxed-warning text (7 product labels, LLM-summarized — see appendix for verbatim text):
All seven labels agree that the sole boxed-warning hazard is metformin-associated lactic acidosis, which has resulted in death, hypothermia, hypotension, and resistant bradyarrhythmias in postmarketing cases; onset is often subtle, with nonspecific symptoms (malaise, myalgias, respiratory distress, somnolence, abdominal pain) and laboratory findings of elevated blood lactate (>5 mmol/L), anion gap acidosis without ketonuria or ketonemia, increased lactate/pyruvate ratio, and metformin plasma levels generally >5 mcg/mL. Risk factors cited across all or most labels include renal impairment, concomitant use of certain drugs (e.g., carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as topiramate), age ≥65 years, radiological studies with contrast, surgery and other procedures, hypoxic states (e.g., acute congestive heart failure), excessive alcohol intake, and hepatic impairment; if lactic acidosis is suspected, the product should be immediately discontinued and prompt hemodialysis initiated in a hospital setting. Two labels differ from the others in a clinically substantive way regarding the risk-factor list: ZITUVIMET and Glumetza explicitly add mitochondrial diseases as an additional named risk factor, whereas the remaining five labels (Metformin Hydrochloride generics and Riomet) do not include mitochondrial diseases in their boxed warnings. Additionally, the abbreviated highlight sections of the Metformin Hydrochloride generic labels (two separate products) express the age cutoff as ">65 years old" rather than "≥65 years old" or "65 years old or greater" used elsewhere, which could be read as excluding 65-year-olds themselves, though the full-text sections of those same labels use the inclusive "65 years old or greater" phrasing.

Open Targets warning metadata: warning type: Black Box Warning; toxicity categories: cardiotoxicity, metabolic toxicity, respiratory toxicity, vascular toxicity.

**Pharmacovigilance:** Open Targets/FAERS pharmacovigilance signals: lactic acidosis (logLR 27686.6; 7697 reports), acute kidney injury (logLR 10158.7; 5387 reports), metabolic acidosis (logLR 6146.0; 2428 reports), hypoglycaemia (logLR 5460.3; 2567 reports), hyperkalaemia (logLR 2224.8; 1327 reports). ## Findings by Disease

### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

#### Literature — Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 8

The evidence on metformin for PCOS is mixed, with controlled trials showing benefit for menstrual regularity, ovulation, and hormonal profiles (PMID: 10634377, PMID: 10206447), while other randomized controlled trials find no improvement in ovulation induction, reproductive outcomes, or metabolic markers (PMID: 11994052, PMID: 16169430, PMID: 19692630). In the specific context of IVF/ICSI, results are also divided, with some trials showing improved pregnancy rates and reduced OHSS (PMID: 16501038) and others showing no benefit in overall IVF outcomes (PMID: 16169430) or only partial benefits without significant pregnancy rate improvement (PMID: 19757275).

**Key findings:**
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled 6-month trial found metformin improved menstrual frequency, reduced free testosterone, and increased insulin sensitivity in PCOS women, with 54.8% of subjects showing striking improvements in menstrual abnormalities in the subsequent open-label phase (PMID: 10634377).
- An uncontrolled study reported that 39 of 43 (91%) previously amenorrheic PCOS women resumed normal menses on metformin, with improvements in testosterone and estradiol independent of weight loss (PMID: 10206447).
- A randomized placebo-controlled double-blind crossover trial in clomiphene-resistant PCOS women found no significant difference between metformin and placebo in spontaneous menstruation, ovulation, or pregnancy rates (PMID: 11994052).
- A prospective randomized trial of metformin in PCOS women undergoing IVF/ICSI found no improvement in IVF/ICSI outcomes (PMID: 16169430).
- A randomized placebo-controlled double-blind IVF trial found no difference in fertilization rates or oocyte yield with metformin, but significantly higher clinical pregnancy rates (38.5% vs 16.3%, P=0.023) and significantly lower severe OHSS incidence (3.8% vs 20.4%, P=0.023) in the metformin group (PMID: 16501038).
- A randomized controlled trial in CC-resistant PCOS women undergoing IVF found metformin improved embryo quality, implantation rate, and reduced OHSS and abortion rates, but did not significantly increase the overall pregnancy rate (PMID: 19757275).
- A small randomized placebo-controlled crossover trial in obese PCOS women found that high-dose metformin (up to 2000 mg/day) did not significantly improve ovulation rates, glucose disposal, serum sex steroids, or other metabolic markers (PMID: 19692630).
- A small prospective randomized study found a non-statistically significant but numerically higher ovulation rate with clomifene plus metformin (62.5%) versus clomifene plus placebo (37.5%), with higher mature follicle numbers and estradiol concentrations in the metformin group (PMID: 19522426).

### Obesity

#### Literature — Obesity

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 9

The preponderance of evidence from randomized controlled trials supports metformin as beneficial for reducing body weight, BMI, and related metabolic parameters in obese non-diabetic individuals, particularly children and adolescents. Multiple double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs demonstrated statistically significant reductions in weight, BMI z-score, body fat, and waist circumference with metformin versus placebo (PMIDs: 11735093, 16595599, 30070925, 9693934, 25483291). The single contradicting RCT (PMID: 20639355) found no BMI benefit but acknowledged a likely underdose and strong placebo/lifestyle effect, and the mixed verdict (PMID: 28759403) showed benefit only in prepubertal children, neither of which offsets the consistent positive signal across the majority of controlled trials.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 24 obese hyperinsulinemic adolescents, metformin produced significantly greater weight loss (6.5% vs 3.8%), greater decrease in body fat, and greater improvements in insulin sensitivity compared to placebo (PMID: 11735093).
- A randomized double-blind crossover trial in 28 obese children aged 9–18 years found metformin had a greater treatment effect over placebo for weight (-4.35 kg), BMI (-1.26 kg/m²), waist circumference (-2.8 cm), subcutaneous abdominal adipose tissue (-52.5 cm²), and fasting insulin (-2.2 mU/liter) (PMID: 16595599).
- A triple-blinded RCT of 150 obese school children found statistically significant reductions in weight, BMI/Age-SDS, fat mass, systolic blood pressure, and lipid parameters in the metformin group versus placebo at 6 and 12 months (PMID: 30070925).
- A double-blind RCT of 30 obese non-diabetic adults found metformin produced significantly greater reductions in body weight (-2.8 vs -0.3 kg), body fat, plasma leptin, and food intake compared to placebo over 15 days (PMID: 9693934).
- A 6-month randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 100 obese hyperinsulinemic children found metformin significantly reduced energy intake after a pre-meal load and decreased hunger ratings while increasing fullness ratings compared to placebo (PMID: 25483291).
- An uncontrolled study of 46 patients with abdominal obesity treated with metformin 2000 mg/day for 3 months showed reductions in body weight (4.2 kg) and waist circumference (4.2 cm) compared to 1.7 kg and 1.1 cm in the comparison group, with improvements in lipid profile and HOMA index (PMID: 25306749).
- A pilot double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 18 pre-pubertal and early pubertal obese children treated with metformin 850 mg/day for 24 months showed trends toward reduction in BMI-SDS, leptin, hsCRP, carotid intima-media thickness, fat mass, and liver fat compared to placebo (PMID: 31821361).
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled multicenter trial in 160 obese children found metformin decreased BMI z-score versus placebo only in prepubertal children (difference 0.2, P=.04), with no significant effect in pubertal children, yielding a mixed verdict (PMID: 28759403).
- A prospective placebo-controlled RCT in 70 obese European adolescents found that both metformin and placebo similarly improved HOMA-IR and fasting insulin with no significant BMI change, with the authors noting the metformin dose (2×500 mg/day) may have been too low (PMID: 20639355).

### Heart Failure

#### Literature — Heart Failure

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 6

The preponderance of evidence from randomized controlled trials and observational studies supports metformin as beneficial in heart failure contexts. A placebo-controlled RCT demonstrated improved myocardial efficiency and reduced myocardial oxygen consumption in non-diabetic HFrEF patients (PMID: 31863557), a controlled trial in non-diabetic HFrEF patients showed improved antioxidant capacity and preserved left ventricular mass index (PMID: 37816240), and a controlled clinical study in CHF patients with type 2 diabetes showed improvements in functional class, hemodynamics, and quality of life with an acceptable safety profile (PMID: 18429758). A systematic review of RCTs confirmed improvements in myocardial oxygen consumption and heart failure markers, though effects on left ventricular function were inconsistent (PMID: 32157481), and a large propensity-matched observational study found metformin associated with significantly reduced all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events in diabetic CKD patients (PMID: 30672083).

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of non-diabetic HFrEF patients, metformin increased myocardial efficiency (work metabolic index) by ~20% and reduced myocardial oxygen consumption compared to placebo (PMID: 31863557).
- In a randomized controlled trial of non-diabetic HFrEF patients, metformin improved total antioxidant capacity and preserved left ventricular mass index compared to standard of care, though changes in LVEF and MDA were not significantly different between groups (PMID: 37816240).
- In a controlled study of CHF patients with type 2 diabetes, metformin use was associated with improvements in NYHA functional class, central hemodynamics, quality of life, reduced CHF decompensations, and sympathoadrenal activation, with no cases of lactic acidosis observed (PMID: 18429758).
- A systematic review of nine RCTs enrolling 2,486 patients found that metformin did not significantly affect left ventricular function but did improve myocardial oxygen consumption and reduced NT-proBNP and LDL levels, with effects inconsistent between diabetic and non-diabetic patients (PMID: 32157481).
- In a propensity-matched analysis of diabetic CKD patients, metformin use was independently associated with reduced all-cause mortality (HR 0.49), cardiovascular death (HR 0.49), and the cardiovascular composite endpoint (HR 0.67), with only two cases of lactic acidosis observed (PMID: 30672083).

### Metabolic Syndrome

#### Literature — Metabolic Syndrome

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 4

The evidence is mixed across comparably credible controlled trials. The large Diabetes Prevention Program randomized trial found metformin reduced metabolic syndrome incidence by 17% versus placebo (PMID: 15838067), while a phase 3 RCT in prostate cancer patients on ADT found no significant reduction in metabolic syndrome prevalence at 18 months (PMID: 40711960), and a smaller Thai RCT found metformin improved some individual parameters but was not superior to placebo overall (PMID: 32342619). A randomized trial of metformin monotherapy showed only 5% normalization of metabolic syndrome biochemical criteria, not meaningfully better than placebo context (PMID: 17924869).

**Key findings:**
- In the Diabetes Prevention Program RCT (n=3234 with impaired glucose tolerance), metformin 850 mg twice daily reduced metabolic syndrome incidence by 17% compared to placebo (P=0.03) over a mean 3.2-year follow-up (PMID: 15838067).
- In the PRIME phase 3 RCT, metformin did not significantly reduce the proportion of patients with metabolic syndrome at 18 months (55% vs 68%, P=0.2) in prostate cancer patients on androgen deprivation therapy, though body weight and waist circumference showed modest improvements (PMID: 40711960).
- In a double-blind RCT of 40 menopausal Thai women, metformin 1700 mg/day for 6 months improved BMI, fasting glucose, and hsCRP within the metformin group, but there was no statistically significant difference in improvement between metformin and placebo groups for cardiovascular risk factors (PMID: 32342619).
- In a randomized trial of 681 patients, metformin monotherapy normalized metabolic syndrome biochemical abnormalities in only 5.0% of patients, compared to 17.4% with high-dose fenofibrate-metformin combination therapy (PMID: 17924869).

### Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Steatotic Liver Disease

#### Literature — Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Steatotic Liver Disease

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The evidence on metformin for MASLD/NAFLD is mixed, with comparably credible controlled trials pointing in both directions. Two randomized controlled trials found no significant improvement in liver histology or steatosis with metformin versus placebo (PMID: 19811343, PMID: 41828627), while two other randomized studies reported improvements in fatty liver prevalence, severity, or steatosis with metformin compared to control conditions (PMID: 18721166, PMID: 20179669). A small active-comparator trial found metformin did not significantly reduce liver fat content versus aerobic exercise (PMID: 24304731), further supporting a null or uncertain effect.

**Key findings:**
- A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 48 biopsy-proven NAFLD patients found no significant differences between metformin and placebo in liver steatosis (histological or CT), NAS score, or liver transaminases after 6 months, though metformin improved body weight, cholesterol, and glycemic markers (PMID: 19811343).
- A 4-arm randomized placebo-controlled trial in 64 MASLD patients found no significant improvement in liver steatosis (CAP or kPa scores) with metformin alone or metformin plus Ganwei compared to placebo at 6 months (PMID: 41828627).
- A randomized study in 50 overweight/obese nondiabetic NAFLD patients found that metformin plus dietary treatment significantly reduced echographic evidence of fatty liver (P<0.0001) and was more effective than dietary treatment alone in normalizing metabolic parameters including HOMA-IR and metabolic syndrome prevalence (PMID: 20179669).
- A randomized trial in 50 obese insulin-resistant adolescents found that metformin plus lifestyle intervention significantly improved fatty liver prevalence (p<0.04), severity (p<0.04), and fasting insulin (p<0.025) compared to placebo plus lifestyle intervention at 6 months (PMID: 18721166).
- A small randomized trial of 16 overweight/obese women found that metformin (1 g/day for 12 weeks) modified liver fat content by 10.37% (CI95% -1.74, 22.48), a non-statistically significant change, while aerobic exercise produced a significant 14.6% reduction (PMID: 24304731).

### Insulin Resistance

#### Literature — Insulin Resistance

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 2

The evidence is mixed regarding metformin's efficacy for treating insulin resistance. One randomized controlled trial found that metformin improved body composition and fasting insulin significantly compared to placebo in obese insulin-resistant children (PMID: 16595599), while another placebo-controlled randomized study found that HOMA-IR and fasting insulin improved similarly in both metformin and placebo groups, with most differences not reaching statistical significance (PMID: 20639355). These two comparably credible controlled trials point in opposite directions with no clear net advantage for metformin over placebo.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized double-blind crossover trial, metformin had a greater treatment effect over placebo for fasting insulin (-2.2 mU/liter, P=0.011) and body composition measures including weight (-4.35 kg, P=0.02) and BMI (-1.26 kg/m², P=0.002) in obese insulin-resistant pediatric patients (PMID: 16595599).
- Insulin sensitivity (Si) improved in 45% of subjects on metformin versus 27% on placebo, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.21) (PMID: 16595599).
- In a prospective placebo-controlled randomized study, HOMA-IR and fasting insulin improved similarly in both the metformin and placebo groups (HOMA-IR decreased 73% vs 54% respectively), with the difference reaching only borderline significance (P=0.048) and most other differences not reaching statistical significance (PMID: 20639355).
- The insulin sensitivity index improved only in the metformin group, but BMI remained unchanged in both groups during the medication period (PMID: 20639355).
- Authors of the contradicting study noted that the metformin dose (2×500 mg/day) may have been too low and that improved lifestyle compliance as a placebo effect may have confounded results (PMID: 20639355).

### Preeclampsia

#### Literature — Preeclampsia

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 6

The evidence on metformin for preeclampsia is mixed across comparably credible controlled studies. A randomized trial in preterm preeclampsia showed a non-significant trend toward prolonged gestation but no difference in composite maternal/neonatal outcomes or angiogenic biomarkers (PMID: 34551918), while a secondary analysis of a randomized trial in diabetic pregnancies found no reduction—and a numerically higher rate—of preterm preeclampsia with metformin versus placebo (PMID: 39236318). A meta-analysis of RCTs found metformin reduced preeclampsia risk versus insulin but not versus placebo (PMID: 29044702), and a broader systematic review concluded metformin was more effective for pregnancy-induced hypertension than for preeclampsia specifically (PMID: 30399639).

**Key findings:**
- In a double-blind RCT of 180 women with preterm preeclampsia, metformin prolonged gestation by a median of 7.6 days versus placebo (p=0.057, non-significant primary endpoint), with no difference in composite maternal, fetal, or neonatal outcomes or angiogenic biomarkers (PMID: 34551918).
- In a secondary analysis of the MOMPOD RCT (n=831 women with early gestational or type 2 diabetes), metformin was not associated with reduced preterm preeclampsia before 37 weeks (aOR 1.04, 95% CI 0.70–1.56) or before 34 weeks (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 0.73–2.81) compared with placebo (PMID: 39236318).
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs comparing metformin versus insulin showed a reduced risk of preeclampsia with metformin (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.48–0.95, p=0.02, I²=0%), but meta-analysis of 5 RCTs comparing metformin versus placebo showed no significant difference (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.33–2.26, p=0.76, I²=66%) (PMID: 29044702).
- A double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in 257 women with PCOS found preeclampsia prevalence was numerically higher in the metformin group (7.4%) than placebo (3.7%), though not statistically significant (p=0.18) (PMID: 20926533).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that metformin showed greater preventive effects for pregnancy-induced hypertension than for preeclampsia specifically, and suggested that dosage, gestational age, and treatment timing require further evaluation (PMID: 30399639).
- A randomized controlled trial in gestational diabetes reported significantly less preeclampsia in metformin-treated groups versus insulin alone, though this was a secondary outcome in a non-blinded study (PMID: 25467617).

### Miscarriage

#### Literature — Miscarriage

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 6

Evidence on metformin for reducing miscarriage is mixed. Several uncontrolled or weakly controlled studies in PCOS populations report significant reductions in miscarriage rates with metformin (PMIDs 11163815, 17162710, 20441061), but the only rigorous RCT specifically examining miscarriage as an outcome in PCOS found no significant difference in miscarriage rates between metformin and placebo (PMID 22419702), and a large RCT in obese pregnant women found no benefit and a non-significant trend toward more adverse pregnancy outcomes including miscarriage in the metformin group (PMID 26165398). The PregMet2 RCT showed a non-significant reduction in the composite of late miscarriage and preterm birth in the primary analysis, with significance only achieved in a post-hoc pooled analysis (PMID 30792154).

**Key findings:**
- In a prospective pilot study of 19 PCOS women, metformin throughout pregnancy reduced first-trimester spontaneous abortion from 73% in prior pregnancies to 10% during metformin use (PMID 11163815).
- In a prospective cohort study of 200 PCOS patients undergoing assisted reproduction, early pregnancy loss was 11.6% in the metformin group versus 36.3% in controls who discontinued metformin (PMID 17162710).
- In an uncontrolled three-group study of 75 pregnant PCOS women, continuing metformin through 12 weeks gestation reduced miscarriage from 32% to 4%, and through 8 weeks from 40% to 8%, compared to prior pregnancies (PMID 20441061).
- In a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT of 320 PCOS women, miscarriage rates were low and similar between metformin (15.2%) and placebo (17.9%, P=0.8), with no significant reduction in miscarriage (PMID 22419702).
- In the EMPOWaR double-blind RCT of 449 obese pregnant women without diabetes, the combined adverse outcome of miscarriage, termination, stillbirth, or neonatal death was numerically higher in the metformin group (n=7) versus placebo (n=2), though not statistically significant (OR 3.60, 95% CI 0.74–17.50, P=0.11) (PMID 26165398).
- In the PregMet2 double-blind RCT of 487 PCOS women, the composite of late miscarriage and preterm birth was 5% with metformin versus 10% with placebo (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.22–1.08, P=0.08), a non-significant result in the primary analysis; significance was only reached in a post-hoc pooled individual participant data analysis (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23–0.79, P=0.004) (PMID 30792154).

### Sepsis

#### Literature — Sepsis

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 5

The preponderance of evidence from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses supports that preadmission metformin use is associated with reduced mortality in diabetic sepsis patients. Two meta-analyses (PMID: 30777119; PMID: 35002982) and an updated larger meta-analysis of 14 studies (PMID: 42087873) consistently report significantly lower overall mortality with metformin use, and a retrospective observational study also found independently lower 90-day and 365-day mortality with post-septic shock metformin administration (PMID: 40394230). The single contradicting study (PMID: 32146543) was a nationwide cohort study that found no significant association with 30-day mortality, but this is outweighed by the consistent positive signal across multiple meta-analyses of higher aggregate evidence.

**Key findings:**
- A meta-analysis of 5 cohort studies (1,282 patients) found metformin use associated with significantly lower mortality in septic diabetic patients (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.43–0.79, P=0.001) (PMID: 30777119).
- A meta-analysis of 11 studies (8,195 patients) found preadmission metformin use associated with lower mortality (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62–0.88, P<0.01) without significant increases in serum creatinine or lactic acid (PMID: 35002982).
- The largest meta-analysis of 14 studies (12,687 patients) found preadmission metformin associated with reduced overall mortality (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.75, P<0.00001), with significant reductions in 28-day, 90-day, 365-day, and in-hospital mortality, though 30-day, 60-day, and ICU mortality did not reach significance (PMID: 42087873).
- A retrospective observational study of 320 septic shock patients with type 2 diabetes found metformin administration within 48 hours independently associated with lower 90-day mortality (aHR 0.371, 95% CI 0.153–0.900, P=0.028) and 365-day mortality (aHR 0.453, 95% CI 0.219–0.937, P=0.033) (PMID: 40394230).
- A large nationwide cohort study of 77,337 diabetic patients found prior metformin therapy was not significantly associated with 30-day mortality after sepsis diagnosis (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.75–1.17, P=0.571) after propensity score adjustment (PMID: 32146543).

### Hyperandrogenism

#### Literature — Hyperandrogenism

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The evidence is mixed regarding metformin's efficacy for hyperandrogenism. Two randomized controlled trials show metformin significantly reduces biochemical androgen markers (testosterone, free androgen index) compared to placebo in adolescents with PCOS and T1D (PMIDs 16520442, 24280743), and one active-comparator trial confirms metformin reduces testosterone and hirsutism scores in PCOS (PMID 23845740). However, one randomized open-label trial found no change in testosterone or free androgen index with metformin over 12 weeks in obese PCOS patients, while rimonabant produced significant reductions (PMID 18410553), and the T1D trial showed biochemical improvements without corresponding clinical benefits such as reduced hirsutism or improved ovulation (PMID 24280743).

**Key findings:**
- Metformin significantly reduced mean serum testosterone by 38.3 ng/dL compared to -0.86 ng/dL with placebo in adolescents with PCOS, and increased the relative risk of menses 2.50-fold (PMID 16520442).
- In adolescents with T1D and hyperandrogenism, metformin significantly decreased testosterone, free androgen index, androstenedione, and 17-OH progesterone compared to placebo, but did not significantly affect clinical parameters such as hirsutism scores or ovulation rates (PMID 24280743).
- In obese PCOS patients, metformin produced no significant change in testosterone or free androgen index after 12 weeks, whereas rimonabant significantly reduced both (PMID 18410553).
- Metformin alone (1700 mg/day) significantly reduced circulating testosterone, androstenedione, and hirsutism scores in PCOS patients, and restored regular menses in approximately 82% of patients after six months (PMID 23845740).
- The addition of low-dose spironolactone to metformin produced a stronger reduction in hirsutism score than metformin alone, suggesting metformin's clinical anti-androgenic effect on hirsutism is limited when used as monotherapy (PMID 23845740).

### Cardiovascular Disorder

#### Literature — Cardiovascular Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 4

The preponderance of evidence supports metformin's benefit for cardiovascular outcomes. A meta-analysis of 61,704 patients with pre-existing CVD found metformin associated with significant reductions in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, coronary revascularisation, and heart failure (PMID: 35032305), and an observational study in CKD patients showed metformin independently associated with reduced all-cause mortality (HR 0.49) and cardiovascular events (HR 0.67) (PMID: 30672083). Mixed signals arise from a meta-analysis showing no overall significant effect on CV events across all comparator types (PMID: 21205121) and a small RCT showing biomarker improvements but no significant change in LVEF (PMID: 37816240), but these do not offset the consistent positive signal from larger, higher-quality evidence.

**Key findings:**
- In a pooled synthesis of 61,704 patients with pre-existing CVD, metformin was associated with reduced all-cause mortality (HR: 0.90), cardiovascular mortality (HR: 0.89), coronary revascularisation (HR: 0.79), and heart failure incidence (HR: 0.90) compared to non-metformin controls (PMID: 35032305).

- A small RCT in 70 nondiabetic HFrEF patients found metformin improved total antioxidant capacity and preserved left ventricular mass index compared to standard of care, but changes in LVEF and MDA were not significantly different between groups (PMID: 37816240).

### Endothelial Dysfunction

#### Literature — Endothelial Dysfunction

**Evidence strength:** moderate, supports
**Relevant studies:** 1

A randomized controlled study found that a single 500 mg dose of metformin prevented the transient endothelial dysfunction caused by an acute glucose load in patients with primary hypertension, as measured by brachial artery flow-mediated vasodilatation (PMID: 23140261). **Key findings:**

- The study concluded that metformin can prevent transient endothelial dysfunction caused by acute glucose load in patients with primary hypertension (PMID: 23140261).

### Atherosclerosis

#### Literature — Atherosclerosis

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 2

The evidence is mixed: a small crossover controlled trial found that metformin significantly improved peripheral arterial blood flow in patients with atherosclerosis (PMID: 6209500), while a larger double-blind RCT (REMOVAL) found that metformin did not significantly reduce progression of mean carotid intima-media thickness (the primary atherosclerosis outcome) in type 1 diabetes patients, though it did significantly reduce maximal cIMT as a tertiary outcome (PMID: 28615149). Comparably credible controlled evidence thus points in both directions, with the larger and more rigorous trial showing a null primary result but some secondary signals of benefit.

**Key findings:**
- Metformin (850 mg tid) significantly increased peripheral arterial blood flow by +17.3% after 3 months and +40.0% after 6 months compared to placebo in a crossover trial of 15 patients with peripheral atherosclerosis (PMID: 6209500).
- The improvement in arterial flow was reversible upon switching to placebo, confirming a drug-specific effect in this controlled experiment (PMID: 6209500).
- In the REMOVAL double-blind RCT (n=428), metformin did not significantly reduce progression of mean far-wall carotid IMT (-0.005 mm per year, 95% CI -0.012 to 0.002; p=0.1664), the primary atherosclerosis endpoint (PMID: 28615149).
- Metformin did significantly reduce maximal cIMT (a prespecified tertiary outcome) by -0.013 mm per year (p=0.0093) in the REMOVAL trial, suggesting a possible but inconsistent anti-atherosclerotic signal (PMID: 28615149).
- Metformin reduced LDL cholesterol (-0.13 mmol/L, p=0.0117) and body weight (-1.17 kg, p<0.0001) over 3 years in the REMOVAL trial, representing favorable cardiovascular risk factor changes (PMID: 28615149).
- Metformin significantly raised HDL cholesterol (+8.3% during treatment) in the peripheral atherosclerosis crossover trial, a potentially beneficial lipid effect (PMID: 6209500).

**Supporting PMIDs:** [6209500](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6209500/), [28615149](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615149/)

**Contradicting PMIDs:** [28615149](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615149/)

⚠️ **Indication-specific safety:** Disease-scoped literature for metformin in atherosclerosis reported: Gastrointestinal adverse effects leading to excess treatment discontinuation — "Discontinuation of treatment in 59 (27%) participants on metformin versus 26 (12%) on placebo (p=0·0002) was mainly due to an excess of gastrointestinal adverse effects" (PMID: [28615149](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615149/)).

### Coronary Artery Disorder

#### Literature — Coronary Artery Disorder

**Evidence strength:** moderate, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 1

A single randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (PMID: 24622715) tested metformin in non-diabetic patients with coronary heart disease and found no significant effect on carotid intima-media thickness progression or carotid plaque score over 18 months, the primary and a key secondary endpoint for cardiovascular disease progression. The evidence therefore contradicts repurposing metformin for coronary artery disorder in non-diabetic patients, and the trial authors concluded that further evidence is needed before metformin can be recommended for cardiovascular benefit in this population (PMID: 24622715).

**Key findings:**
- In a double-blind RCT of non-diabetic patients with coronary heart disease, metformin (850 mg twice daily) did not significantly reduce progression of mean distal carotid intima-media thickness compared with placebo (slope difference 0.007 mm/year, 95% CI −0.006 to 0.020; p=0.29) over 18 months (PMID: 24622715).
- Change in carotid plaque score also did not differ significantly between metformin and placebo groups (0.01 per year, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.26; p=0.92) (PMID: 24622715).
- Metformin reduced HbA1c, insulin, HOMA-IR, and tissue plasminogen activator compared with placebo, but had no significant effect on total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, non-HDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, or high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (PMID: 24622715).
- Diarrhoea and nausea or vomiting were more common in the metformin group than in the placebo group (28 vs 5 events) (PMID: 24622715).

### Breast Cancer

#### Literature — Breast Cancer

**Evidence strength:** strong, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 7

The totality of clinical evidence from randomized controlled trials does not support metformin as an effective treatment for breast cancer. The large phase III MA.32 trial found no improvement in invasive disease-free survival or overall survival with metformin versus placebo in non-diabetic patients with high-risk operable breast cancer (PMID: 35608580), and a secondary analysis of the same trial showed metformin did not reduce new primary cancer development (PMID: 37695982). A phase II randomized trial in metastatic breast cancer similarly found no significant benefit in progression-free survival, overall survival, or response rate (PMID: 31472446). Biomarker-level presurgical trials showed no significant modulation of apoptosis (PMID: 24157825) and no overall effect on proliferation, with only a subgroup signal in HER2-positive DCIS that is insufficient to offset the negative controlled clinical evidence (PMID: 26276754).

**Key findings:**
- In the MA.32 phase III RCT (n=3649), metformin versus placebo did not improve invasive disease-free survival in ER/PgR-positive patients (HR 1.01; 95% CI 0.84–1.21; P=0.93) or overall survival (HR 1.10; 95% CI 0.86–1.41; P=0.47), and grade 3 non-hematological toxic events were more frequent with metformin (PMID: 35608580).
- A secondary analysis of MA.32 found that metformin did not reduce the risk of new primary invasive cancers, with numerically more new cancers in the metformin arm (102 vs. 82; HR 1.25; 95% CI 0.94–1.68; P=0.13) (PMID: 37695982).
- In a phase II double-blind RCT of metformin plus chemotherapy versus placebo plus chemotherapy in metastatic breast cancer (n=40), mean PFS was 5.4 vs. 6.3 months (HR 1.2; 95% CI 0.63–2.31) and mean OS was 20.2 vs. 24.2 months (HR 1.68; 95% CI 0.79–3.55), with no significant benefit on any clinical endpoint (PMID: 31472446).
- A randomized presurgical trial found no significant difference in apoptosis (TUNEL) between metformin and placebo arms overall (P=0.2), with only a non-significant trend toward differential effects by insulin resistance status (PMID: 24157825).
- A randomized presurgical trial found metformin did not significantly affect Ki-67 proliferation in DCIS or ductal hyperplasia overall, though a subgroup of HER2-positive/ER-positive DCIS showed lower Ki-67 with metformin (12% vs. 32%; P=0.004), representing a limited biomarker signal in a small subgroup (PMID: 26276754).

WARNING: LACTIC ACIDOSIS Postmarketing cases of metformin-associated lactic acidosis have resulted in death, hypothermia, hypotension, and resistant bradyarrhythmias. The onset of metformin-associated lactic acidosis is often subtle, accompanied only by nonspecific symptoms such as malaise, myalgias, respiratory distress, somnolence, and abdominal pain. Metformin-associated lactic acidosis was characterized by elevated blood lactate levels (> 5 mmol/Liter), anion gap acidosis (without evidence of ketonuria or ketonemia), an increased lactate/pyruvate ratio; and metformin plasma levels generally > 5 mcg/mL [see Warnings and Precautions (5.1) ] . Risk factors for metformin-associated lactic acidosis include renal impairment, concomitant use of certain drugs (e.g., carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as topiramate), age 65 years old or greater, having a radiological study with contrast, surgery and other procedures, hypoxic states (e.g., acute congestive heart failure), excessive alcohol intake, and hepatic impairment. Steps to reduce the risk of and manage metformin-associated lactic acidosis in these high risk groups are provided [see Dosage and Administration (2.3) , Contraindications (4) , Warnings and Precautions (5.1) ] .
