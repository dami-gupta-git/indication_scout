## Findings by Disease

### Binge Eating Disorder

#### Literature — Binge Eating Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

Multiple randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials have evaluated bupropion (as part of the naltrexone/bupropion combination) for binge-eating disorder (BED), yielding mixed results. Bupropion monotherapy did not significantly reduce binge-eating frequency relative to placebo in overweight/obese women with BED, though it produced modest weight loss (PMID: 23656848). Naltrexone/bupropion combination similarly failed to significantly reduce binge-eating frequency versus placebo in two trials (PMID: 37751990, PMID: 33218742), but one larger trial found naltrexone-bupropion significantly superior to placebo for binge-eating remission, particularly when combined with behavioral weight loss therapy (PMID: 36285406), and a maintenance trial suggested naltrexone/bupropion helped sustain remission in acute responders (PMID: 37366017).

**Key findings:**
- Bupropion monotherapy (300 mg/day, 8 weeks) did not significantly reduce binge-eating frequency, food craving, or eating disorder psychopathology compared to placebo in overweight/obese women with BED, though it produced significantly greater weight loss (1.8% vs 0.6% BMI loss, P=.002) (PMID: 23656848).
- In a 12-week RCT, naltrexone/bupropion did not significantly reduce binge-eating frequency or remission rates compared to placebo in patients with BED, but was associated with significantly greater percentage weight loss and higher rates of ≥5% weight loss (27.9% vs 6.5%) (PMID: 37751990).
- A pilot RCT of naltrexone/bupropion (NB 50/300 mg, 12 weeks) found significant reductions from baseline in binge-eating, eating-disorder psychopathology, depression, and weight, but these changes did not differ significantly from placebo; the percentage attaining ≥3% weight loss was significantly greater with NB than placebo (45.5% vs 0%) (PMID: 33218742).
- In a 16-week randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial using a 2×2 factorial design, naltrexone-bupropion was significantly superior to placebo for binge-eating remission (31.3% vs 17.7%), and the combination of BWL+naltrexone-bupropion achieved the highest remission rate (57.1%); there was no significant interaction between BWL and medication (PMID: 36285406).
- In a maintenance RCT among responders to acute treatments, naltrexone/bupropion maintenance was associated with binge-eating remission rates of 68.8% versus 50.0% for placebo; placebo following acute naltrexone/bupropion was associated with significantly decreased probability of binge-eating remission and increased binge-eating frequency (PMID: 37366017).
- Naltrexone/bupropion maintenance following acute naltrexone/bupropion response was associated with good maintenance of binge-eating remission, low binge-eating frequency, and significant additional weight loss (PMID: 37366017).

### Attention Deficit-hyperactivity Disorder

#### Literature — Attention Deficit-hyperactivity Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 13

Multiple randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled and active-comparator trials demonstrate that bupropion is effective for treating ADHD in both children and adults. Bupropion produced significantly greater reductions in ADHD symptoms compared to placebo in adults (PMID: 11156812, PMID: 15820237, PMID: 25325205) and in children (PMID: 8885585), and showed comparable efficacy to methylphenidate in head-to-head trials (PMID: 7775360, PMID: 22806822). One trial in methadone-maintained patients with comorbid cocaine dependence found no significant advantage of bupropion over placebo on ADHD outcomes, representing a contradicting result in a specialized population (PMID: 16102908), and one adult trial showed a non-significant trend favoring bupropion over placebo (PMID: 11791949), introducing some mixed signals.

**Key findings:**
- In a 6-week double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in adults with DSM-IV ADHD, bupropion SR produced a 42% reduction in ADHD symptoms vs. 24% for placebo, with 76% of bupropion-treated subjects responding vs. 37% on placebo (PMID: 11156812).
- In a multisite, 8-week placebo-controlled RCT, bupropion XL responders (53%) significantly exceeded placebo responders (31%; p=.004), with an effect size of 0.6 on the ADHD Rating Scale total score (PMID: 15820237).
- In a 6-week double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in adults, bupropion 150 mg/day produced a significantly greater reduction in CAARS scores compared to placebo after 6 weeks (PMID: 25325205).
- In a multisite, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in children aged 6–12 with ADDH, bupropion showed significant treatment effects on conduct problems and hyperactivity on teacher and parent Conners scales as early as day 3, and on Continuous Performance Test and memory retrieval (PMID: 8885585).
- In a double-blind crossover trial in youth aged 7–17, bupropion and methylphenidate both produced significantly greater and equivalent improvement on the Iowa-Conners Teacher's Rating Scale, CGI, and neuropsychological measures (PMID: 7775360).
- In a 6-week randomized double-blind study in children and adolescents, bupropion showed no significant difference from methylphenidate on Parent and Teacher ADHD-RS-IV scores, with 90% response rate on the Parent scale in both groups (PMID: 22806822).
- In a 7-week randomized double-blind parallel trial in adults, response rates were 64% for bupropion SR, 50% for methylphenidate, and 27% for placebo, though the difference between active treatment and placebo did not reach statistical significance (p=0.14) (PMID: 11791949).
- In a 12-week double-blind three-arm trial in methadone-maintained patients with adult ADHD, sustained-release bupropion did not provide a significant advantage over placebo in reducing ADHD symptoms, with a high placebo response rate of 46% (PMID: 16102908).
- An open trial of bupropion in 19 adults with ADHD found that 14 of 19 patients experienced moderate to marked benefit, and 10 chose to continue bupropion over their prior medication (PMID: 2115746).
- A 12-week single-blind trial of bupropion in 11 patients with cocaine dependence and adult ADHD found significant reductions in attention difficulties, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, with improvements similar to those previously observed with sustained-release methylphenidate (PMID: 11916368).
- A 5-week open-label bupropion trial in 13 adolescent boys with ADHD, conduct disorder, and substance use disorders found significant declines in Conners Hyperactivity Index (75.5 to 65.4, p<.01), Daydream Attention scores (p<.02), and CGI-S ratings (p<.002) (PMID: 9847499).
- Bupropion at 150 mg/day in healthy volunteers increased composedness, agreeability, and energy on the POMS scale, while 300 mg/day improved attention, suggesting psychotropic effects relevant to ADHD symptom domains (PMID: 12826985).
- Reanalysis of cardiovascular data from placebo-controlled ADHD trials found that bupropion was associated with statistically significant increases in systolic blood pressure (+5.9 mm Hg, p<.05) and heart rate (+6.9 mm Hg, p<.05) compared to baseline in adults with ADHD (PMID: 15705013).

### Cocaine Use Disorder

#### Literature — Cocaine Use Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 9

The evidence for bupropion in cocaine use disorder is mixed across multiple study types. A systematic review and meta-analysis found low-strength evidence that bupropion increased abstinence rates (RR 1.63) in two RCTs (PMID: 31183685), and a large retrospective cohort study found bupropion associated with higher CUD remission rates (HR 1.57) (PMID: 37703770), while a human laboratory RCT found that naltrexone-bupropion combinations did not significantly decrease cocaine self-administration (PMID: 36805862) and a clinical RCT found no overall benefit of bupropion SR over placebo on cocaine abstinence, though a subgroup benefit was observed in abstinence-initiation patients (PMID: 36920397). Animal studies show bupropion attenuates cocaine self-administration at high doses in rats (PMID: 39881654) but produced stimulant-like effects at doses needed to reduce nicotine+cocaine co-use in nonhuman primates (PMID: 31789555).

**Key findings:**
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 2 RCTs found bupropion was associated with increased cocaine abstinence compared to placebo (RR 1.63, 95% CI 1.02–2.59), though the strength of evidence was rated as low (PMID: 31183685).
- A narrative review identified bupropion as among the medications showing the most promise for cocaine use disorder, alongside psychostimulants, modafinil, topiramate, and disulfiram, but noted evidence is limited by heterogeneity and small sample sizes (PMID: 31008728).
- A review of bupropion and its analogs noted clinical studies supporting bupropion's benefit in cocaine dependence and described development of bupropion analogs as potential pharmacotherapies for cocaine addiction (PMID: 24484978).
- A human laboratory RCT found that bupropion (0, 100, 200, 400 mg/day) alone or combined with naltrexone did not significantly decrease cocaine self-administration on either a progressive ratio or relapse procedure in 31 participants with cocaine use disorder; low-dose bupropion (100 mg) blunted subjective 'Like Drug' and 'Stimulated' ratings (PMID: 36805862).
- A 26-week double-blind RCT found no significant overall difference in cocaine abstinence between bupropion SR and placebo when combined with financial incentives; however, in the abstinence-initiation subgroup, bupropion SR produced significantly more cocaine-negative samples (30.3 vs 17.1) and higher abstinence at end of study (66.7% vs 30.0%, P=.04) (PMID: 36920397).
- A large retrospective cohort study (n=161,544 patients with CUD and depression) found bupropion was associated with higher CUD remission rates compared to other antidepressants (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.27–1.94), and in CUD patients with co-occurring nicotine dependence, bupropion showed higher remission than other nicotine dependence drugs (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.11–1.71) (PMID: 37703770).
- A health claims retrospective analysis found bupropion prescription increased odds of stimulant use disorder remission 2.1-fold in amphetamine use disorder but NOT in cocaine use disorder specifically (PMID: 30815171).
- In male and female Wistar rats, systemic bupropion at 60 mg/kg attenuated cocaine self-administration during extended-access (6-hour) sessions without altering locomotor activity or inactive lever presses; females showed greater efficacy than males during the first hour (PMID: 39881654).
- In nonhuman primates, bupropion treatment dose-dependently decreased self-administration of nicotine combined with a low cocaine dose, but at the effective dosage also increased responding for cocaine alone, nicotine alone, and saline, and significantly increased daily activity, suggesting stimulant-like effects that do not support clinical use for nicotine+cocaine polydrug abuse (PMID: 31789555).

### Bipolar Disorder

#### Literature — Bipolar Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 15

Multiple controlled and open-label trials indicate that bupropion has antidepressant efficacy in bipolar depression when used adjunctively with mood stabilizers, with response rates of approximately 49–59% in several studies (PMID: 16880481, PMID: 12180276, PMID: 10701483), and it appears to carry a lower risk of inducing manic switch compared to venlafaxine and desipramine (PMID: 7929019, PMID: 16880481, PMID: 16449476). However, the evidence is mixed: one large double-blind placebo-controlled trial found no significant benefit of adjunctive antidepressant therapy (including bupropion) over mood stabilizer plus placebo for durable recovery (PMID: 17392295), and several studies document meaningful rates of manic/hypomanic switching with bupropion use, as well as cases where bupropion precipitated manic episodes (PMID: 1487473, PMID: 11912569, PMID: 37530824). Overall, the evidence supports a potential role for bupropion in bipolar depression but with important caveats regarding switch risk and uncertain long-term benefit.

**Key findings:**
- In a double-blind trial, bupropion added to lithium or anticonvulsant therapy showed similar antidepressant efficacy to desipramine, but mania/hypomania occurred in only 1 of 9 bupropion-treated patients versus 5 of 10 desipramine-treated patients (PMID: 7929019).
- In a 10-week randomized trial of 174 bipolar depressed patients, bupropion, sertraline, and venlafaxine showed similar acute response rates (49–53%) and remission rates (34–41%), but venlafaxine was associated with a significantly higher risk of switching into hypomania or mania compared to bupropion or sertraline (PMID: 16880481).
- In a single-blind study, bupropion SR added to mood stabilizer therapy produced a response rate of 59% (≥50% reduction in HDRS-17) in bipolar I/II depression, with no cases of affective switch observed (PMID: 12180276).
- In a double-blind study comparing idazoxan and bupropion in bipolar I depressed patients, both drugs produced approximately 50% reductions in Hamilton Depression scores over 6 weeks (PMID: 10701483).
- An open trial of bupropion SR in adults with ADHD plus bipolar disorder found significant reductions in ADHD symptoms and also reductions in ratings of mania and depression, without significant activation of mania (PMID: 12842303).
- An open trial of bupropion in 11 patients with bipolar or schizoaffective disorder found that most patients maintained on bupropion alone or in combination for 1 year or more showed little or no relapse and few side effects (PMID: 2856918).
- A small open-label add-on study in 12 depressed bipolar I patients with comorbid cocaine dependence found that bupropion augmentation improved Hamilton Depression Rating Scale scores and Drug Abuse Screening Test scores after 4 weeks, with no significant increase in Young Mania Rating Scale scores (PMID: 24434527).
- In a large double-blind placebo-controlled trial (STEP-BD), adjunctive antidepressant therapy (including bupropion) plus mood stabilizer did not significantly outperform mood stabilizer plus placebo for durable recovery (23.5% vs. 27.3%, P=0.40), with similar rates of treatment-emergent affective switch in both groups (PMID: 17392295).
- In a randomized double-blind prospective study of bupropion, sertraline, and venlafaxine as adjuncts to mood stabilizers, 14% of acute treatment phases and 33% of continuation phases were associated with switches into hypomania or mania across all three antidepressants (PMID: 11912569).
- In 228 acute randomized trials of adjunctive bupropion, sertraline, or venlafaxine in bipolar depression, threshold switches into full-duration hypomania and mania occurred in 11.4% and 7.9% of acute trials respectively; venlafaxine had the highest relative risk of switching and bupropion the lowest (PMID: 16449476).
- In a blinded continuation study, 13% of acute positive antidepressant responders (treated with bupropion, sertraline, or venlafaxine adjunctive to mood stabilizers) developed mania during the continuation phase, and the switch rate was not higher than reported rates for mood stabilizer monotherapy (PMID: 19358785).
- A post-hoc analysis of STEP-BD data found that higher doses of adjunctive antidepressants were associated with greater durable recovery than low doses, without a significant increase in treatment-emergent affective switch (PMID: 26228414).
- A double-blind maintenance trial of adjunctive escitalopram or bupropion XL in bipolar I disorder found that continuing antidepressants for 52 weeks did not significantly reduce any mood episode compared to 8 weeks (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.43–1.10, P=0.12), though depression recurrence was lower in the 52-week group (17% vs. 40%); mania/hypomania was numerically higher in the 52-week group (12% vs. 6%) (PMID: 37530824).
- A case series of 11 bipolar patients treated with bupropion found that 7 of 11 had moderate-to-marked improvement after 6 weeks, but 6 of 11 experienced manic or hypomanic episodes necessitating discontinuation, suggesting bupropion may pose similar risks as other antidepressants for precipitating mania (PMID: 1487473).
- Carbamazepine markedly decreased bupropion peak concentrations by 87% and 24-hour AUC by 90%, while valproate did not significantly affect bupropion concentrations, indicating a clinically important pharmacokinetic interaction when combining bupropion with carbamazepine in bipolar disorder treatment (PMID: 8830063).

### Alcohol Dependence

#### Literature — Alcohol Dependence

**Evidence strength:** weak, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 2

Preclinical and animal evidence suggests bupropion may reduce alcohol consumption without worsening relapse. In a rat model of alcohol self-administration and relapse, bupropion treatment during abstinence did not increase alcohol consumption during relapse, unlike fluoxetine which produced a long-lasting increase (PMID: 31064683). In male C57BL/6J mice, bupropion dose-dependently reduced binge-like ethanol intake and chronic intermittent ethanol intake, and subthreshold doses of bupropion combined with naltrexone also reduced binge-like drinking (PMID: 30817015). No human clinical trial evidence directly testing bupropion as a treatment for alcohol dependence was identified in the provided abstracts.

**Key findings:**
- In a rat model, bupropion (20 mg/kg, 14-day treatment during abstinence) did not increase alcohol consumption during relapse, in contrast to fluoxetine which produced a long-lasting increase in alcohol consumption (PMID: 31064683).
- Bupropion treatment was associated with distinct alterations in glutamatergic and endocannabinoid signaling in the prefrontal cortex compared to fluoxetine, suggesting a different mechanistic profile relevant to alcohol relapse (PMID: 31064683).
- Bupropion (20 and 40 mg/kg i.p.) dose-dependently blunted binge-like ethanol intake in male C57BL/6J mice using 'drinking in the dark' procedures (PMID: 30817015).
- Bupropion (20, 40, and 60 mg/kg i.p.) dose-dependently reduced ethanol intake in mice following 16 weeks of intermittent access to ethanol (PMID: 30817015).
- Subthreshold doses of bupropion (20 mg/kg) combined with naltrexone (3 mg/kg) reduced binge-like ethanol intake in mice, suggesting a potential combination approach (PMID: 30817015).
- Bupropion failed to reduce consumption of a 3% sucrose solution in mice, suggesting the effect on ethanol intake is not due to general appetite suppression (PMID: 30817015).

### Neuropathic Pain

#### Literature — Neuropathic Pain

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 4

Evidence for bupropion in neuropathic pain is mixed. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 41 nondepressed patients found that bupropion SR (150–300 mg/day) significantly reduced neuropathic pain scores compared to placebo, with 73% of patients reporting improvement (PMID: 11706096); a narrative review also noted bupropion shows promise for neuropathic pain via noradrenaline and dopamine reuptake inhibition (PMID: 20185402). However, a separate randomized placebo-controlled crossover trial found no significant benefit of bupropion SR over placebo in patients with non-neuropathic chronic low back pain, suggesting efficacy may be specific to neuropathic rather than nociceptive pain (PMID: 16202958). Preclinical data in a rat spinal nerve ligation model demonstrated dose-dependent antihyperalgesic effects of intrathecal bupropion mediated through spinal noradrenaline and dopamine pathways (PMID: 25427287).

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of 41 nondepressed patients, bupropion SR reduced mean average pain scores by 1.7 points (from 5.7 to 4.0, p<0.001) versus no change with placebo, with 73% of patients reporting improvement or much improvement (PMID: 11706096).
- Pain relief with bupropion SR was statistically significant from week 2 onward (p<0.05 at week 2; p<0.001 at weeks 3–6), and a significant decrease in pain interference with quality of life was observed (PMID: 11706096).
- Side effects of bupropion SR in the neuropathic pain trial were not dose-limiting and included dry mouth, insomnia, headache, gastrointestinal upset, tremor, constipation, and dizziness (PMID: 11706096).
- A randomized, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 44 patients with predominantly non-neuropathic chronic low back pain found no significant difference between bupropion SR and placebo on daily pain intensity ratings, weekly pain intensity ratings, McGill Pain Questionnaire Present Pain Intensity scale, or pain relief ratings (PMID: 16202958).
- Bupropion is described as a specific inhibitor of neuronal noradrenaline reuptake and a weak inhibitor of dopamine reuptake, and shows some promise in the treatment of neuropathic pain (PMID: 20185402).
- Intrathecal bupropion produced a dose-dependent antihyperalgesic effect in a rat spinal nerve ligation model (3, 10, 30, and 100 µg, p<0.001), and this effect was reversed by intrathecal α2-adrenoceptor antagonist idazoxan and D2 receptor antagonist sulpiride (PMID: 25427287).
- In vivo microdialysis showed that intrathecal bupropion (30 µg) significantly increased noradrenaline and dopamine concentrations in the spinal dorsal horn of rats with neuropathic pain (p<0.001 and p=0.001, respectively) (PMID: 25427287).

### Post-traumatic Stress Disorder

#### Literature — Post-traumatic Stress Disorder

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 3

The available drug-specific evidence does not support repurposing bupropion for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). A placebo-controlled RCT of bupropion SR in 30 patients with chronic PTSD found no significant between-group differences in PTSD symptom reduction compared to placebo, though a post hoc analysis suggested younger patients not on concurrent antidepressants might benefit (PMID: 17414245). A systematic review and meta-analysis similarly found that bupropion failed to differentiate from placebo in PTSD treatment (PMID: 27126398). A small case series of four patients with comorbid PTSD and methamphetamine use disorder reported greater reductions in PTSD symptom severity with bupropion-containing regimens compared to serotonergic agents alone, but this was a very small, uncontrolled report with significant methodological limitations (PMID: 39175424).

**Key findings:**
- In an 8-week placebo-controlled RCT of bupropion SR (n=30, 2:1 randomization), no significant between-group differences in PTSD symptom reduction were detected; both bupropion SR and placebo groups showed symptom reduction (PMID: 17414245).
- A post hoc analysis of the bupropion SR RCT suggested younger patients not currently on another antidepressant may be more likely to benefit, but this was hypothesis-generating only (PMID: 17414245).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of PTSD pharmacotherapy found that bupropion failed to differentiate from placebo, placing it among medications that did not demonstrate efficacy for PTSD (PMID: 27126398).
- A case series of four patients with comorbid PTSD and methamphetamine use disorder reported PTSD Checklist-Civilian scores improving from 77 at initiation to 35 at discharge and 29 post-discharge with bupropion-containing regimens, compared to minimal change (51 to 52 to 53) in a non-bupropion control group of 41 patients (PMID: 39175424).
- The case series also reported lower relapse rates (25.0% vs 48.8%) and lower hospital utilization (0% vs 29.3%) in the bupropion group versus the non-bupropion group, though missing data and small sample size limited conclusions (PMID: 39175424).

### Parkinson Disease

#### Literature — Parkinson Disease

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The available evidence suggests that bupropion has some utility in Parkinson's disease, primarily for treating comorbid depression and neuropsychiatric symptoms, with mixed findings on motor symptoms. A small clinical trial in 20 PD patients found that bupropion reduced parkinsonism by at least 30% in half the patients but alleviated depression in only 5 of 12 depressed patients, with frequent dose-limiting side effects (PMID: 6431314). Case reports and a scoping review indicate bupropion can effectively reduce depressive symptoms and apathy in PD patients, though controlled evidence is lacking and the effect on motor symptoms remains controversial (PMID: 20809995, PMID: 35513785, PMID: 11086491). A hypothesis paper proposes bupropion as a first-line antidepressant in PD based on its dopaminergic and noradrenergic mechanism, but acknowledges the absence of controlled clinical trials (PMID: 20708340).

**Key findings:**
- In a clinical evaluation of 20 idiopathic PD patients, bupropion reduced parkinsonism by at least 30% in half the patients, but depression was alleviated in only 5 of 12 depressed patients; side effects were frequent and dose-limiting in five patients (PMID: 6431314).
- A scoping review of 23 articles found bupropion showed considerable effectiveness in reducing depressive symptoms in PD, particularly apathy, with a positive safety profile, but concluded that available literature lacks good evidence to support its use and further investigations are needed (PMID: 35513785).
- A case report described a 78-year-old female with PD whose depression improved after a trial of bupropion following unsuccessful treatment with other antidepressants, with four additional reports of successful treatment of depression in PD with bupropion identified; no controlled double-blind studies have been conducted (PMID: 20809995).
- A case report described a 70-year-old female PD patient with medication-resistant depression who achieved quick and complete remission of depressive symptoms with bupropion without any negative effects on motor symptoms (PMID: 11086491).
- A hypothesis paper proposed bupropion (NDRI) as a first-line treatment for depression in PD patients based on its dual norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake inhibition mechanism, but acknowledged that clinical controlled studies are required to support this hypothesis (PMID: 20708340).

### Narcolepsy

#### Literature — Narcolepsy

**Evidence strength:** weak, supports
**Relevant studies:** 2

Two case reports describe bupropion improving symptoms in narcolepsy patients. In one case, bupropion (100 mg three times daily) normalized mood, sleepiness (mean sleep latency improved from 1.8 to 9.1 minutes), and REM-sleep propensity in a narcoleptic woman with atypical depression, with reversion upon discontinuation (PMID: 9614600). In a second case, bupropion 300 mg/day rendered a narcolepsy-cataplexy patient with comorbid depression asymptomatic after 3 months, following failure of imipramine and modafinil (PMID: 24930928). The evidence supports potential utility of bupropion in narcolepsy but is limited to individual case reports.

**Key findings:**
- Bupropion 100 mg three times daily normalized sleepiness (mean sleep latency improved from 1.8 to 9.1 minutes), REM-sleep propensity, and mood (Beck Depression Inventory score improved from 24 to 6) in a 46-year-old narcoleptic woman with atypical depression; all parameters reverted to pretreatment levels upon discontinuation (PMID: 9614600).
- The authors of PMID 9614600 proposed that bupropion's reversal of sleepiness and REM-sleep propensity in narcolepsy may be due to blockade of dopamine or norepinephrine reuptake, and called for further investigation in placebo-controlled trials (PMID: 9614600).
- Bupropion 300 mg/day rendered a 58-year-old woman with narcolepsy-cataplexy syndrome and comorbid depression (Epworth Sleepiness Scale score 14, Beck Depression Inventory score 29) asymptomatic after 3 months, after prior failure of imipramine and modafinil (PMID: 24930928).
- The authors of PMID 24930928 noted this was the second reported case of a depressive narcoleptic patient responding to 300 mg/day of bupropion (PMID: 24930928).

### Multiple Sclerosis

#### Literature — Multiple Sclerosis

**Evidence strength:** weak, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 3

The evidence for repurposing bupropion to treat multiple sclerosis itself is limited and mixed. A single-patient case report found that bupropion improved MS-related chronic fatigue as measured by the Fatigue Severity Scale, with discontinuation and reinitiation confirming the effect (PMID: 21118738). A clinical study found that lower reward responsiveness at baseline predicted higher fatigue remission rates in bupropion-treated MS patients compared to escitalopram-treated patients, suggesting bupropion may benefit a subgroup of fatigued MS patients (PMID: 22723570). However, a FAERS disproportionality analysis found no inverse association between bupropion and MS, unlike several anti-diabetic weight-loss drugs, providing no signal for bupropion as a disease-modifying agent in MS (PMID: 38566910).

**Key findings:**
- A 47-year-old woman with relapsing-remitting MS experienced improvement in chronic fatigue (FSS score from 7 to 4) after 3 months of bupropion 300 mg/d, with discontinuation and reinitiation confirming the effect; no changes in depression, sleep, or daytime sleepiness scores were observed (PMID: 21118738).
- In a study of 104 fatigued and 70 fatigue-free relapsing-remitting MS patients, lower reward responsiveness at baseline predicted higher fatigue remission rates in bupropion-treated patients compared to escitalopram-treated patients (PMID: 22723570).
- Fatigued MS patients demonstrated lower reward responsiveness compared to fatigue-free MS patients, and reward responsiveness scores were associated with baseline Modified Fatigue Impact Scale scores (PMID: 22723570).
- A FAERS disproportionality analysis found no inverse association between bupropion and MS (unlike semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, empagliflozin, and metformin), providing no pharmacovigilance signal supporting bupropion repurposing for MS (PMID: 38566910).

### Autism Spectrum Disorder

#### Literature — Autism Spectrum Disorder

**Evidence strength:** weak, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 3

The evidence for bupropion in autism spectrum disorder is limited and indirect. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that evidence for bupropion in treating ADHD symptoms in ASD individuals is 'either preliminary or nonexistent' (PMID: 39004333). A Cochrane review on pharmacological interventions for irritability, aggression, and self-injury in ASD grouped antidepressants (which would include bupropion) versus placebo and found no clear evidence that antidepressants affect irritability in ASD, with some signals of increased adverse effects (PMID: 37811711). A broader pediatric psychopharmacology review of ASD noted that for several drugs including bupropion, only case reports and open-label studies suggest possible efficacy with no randomized controlled trials yet performed (PMID: 33857522). Overall, the evidence neither clearly supports nor clearly refutes bupropion for ASD, but is too sparse and indirect to draw firm conclusions.

**Key findings:**
- A systematic review and meta-analysis found that evidence for bupropion as an intervention for ADHD symptoms in ASD individuals is 'either preliminary or nonexistent,' with bupropion mentioned alongside clonidine and modafinil as having only a few articles and insufficient evidence compared to methylphenidate or atomoxetine (PMID: 39004333).
- A Cochrane review on pharmacological interventions for irritability, aggression, and self-injury in ASD found no clear evidence that antidepressants (as a class, including bupropion) have an effect on irritability in ASD (SMD -0.06, 95% CI -0.30 to 0.18; 3 studies, 267 participants; low-certainty evidence), and noted that rates of neurological and psychological adverse events may be higher with antidepressants (PMID: 37811711).
- A systematic review of pediatric psychopharmacology in ASD noted that for several drugs, case reports and open-label studies suggest possible efficacy but no randomized controlled trial has yet been performed, implying bupropion lacks robust trial-level evidence for ASD (PMID: 33857522).
