## Drug Safety

_Drug-wide safety signal — applies to the drug generally, not to any single candidate indication below._

**Regulatory label:** FDA label boxed-warning text (17 product labels, LLM-summarized — see appendix for verbatim text):
All 17 labels agree that the core boxed-warning hazard is metformin-associated lactic acidosis, a serious and potentially fatal complication characterized by elevated blood lactate levels (>5 mmol/L), anion gap acidosis without ketonuria or ketonemia, an increased lactate/pyruvate ratio, and metformin plasma levels generally >5 mcg/mL, with postmarketing cases resulting in death, hypothermia, hypotension, and resistant bradyarrhythmias; onset is often subtle, with nonspecific symptoms including malaise, myalgias, respiratory distress, somnolence, and abdominal pain. Shared risk factors across nearly all labels include renal impairment, hepatic impairment, hypoxic states, excessive alcohol intake, radiological studies with contrast, and surgery or other procedures; management in all labels requires immediate discontinuation of metformin, hospitalization with general supportive measures, and prompt hemodialysis. Two substantive differences exist among the labels: the older-format labels (covering GLUCOPHAGE/GLUCOPHAGE XR, FORTAMET, GLUMETZA [older version], the unnamed metformin HCl USP generics, and the Glipizide/Metformin combination product) specify an age-based prescribing restriction — that metformin should not be initiated in patients ≥80 years of age unless creatinine clearance confirms preserved renal function — a restriction absent from the newer-format labels, which instead list age ≥65 years as a general risk factor without imposing an initiation cutoff at 80; and the ZITUVIMET label and the newer Glumetza label explicitly add mitochondrial diseases as an additional risk factor for lactic acidosis, a hazard not mentioned in any of the other 15 labels. The one unnamed GLUMETZA label using the older short-form boxed warning (listing only sepsis, dehydration, excess alcohol, hepatic insufficiency, renal impairment, and acute congestive heart failure) is also substantively narrower than the other labels in its enumeration of risk factors, omitting age, contrast studies, surgery, and drug interactions from the boxed warning text itself.

## Findings by Disease

### Preeclampsia

#### Literature — Preeclampsia

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 14

The evidence on metformin for preeclampsia prevention or treatment is mixed across study designs and populations. Some meta-analyses and RCTs report a reduced risk of preeclampsia with metformin versus insulin (PMIDs 29044702, 33927270, 38124287, 40852348), while controlled trials and large cohort analyses comparing metformin to placebo find no significant reduction in preeclampsia incidence (PMIDs 20926533, 39236318, 39334302, 42827236, 42965204). The one RCT directly testing metformin as a treatment in established preterm preeclampsia showed a borderline non-significant prolongation of gestation in the primary analysis (PMID 34551918), leaving the net clinical benefit uncertain.

**Key findings:**
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 180 women with preterm preeclampsia found a median prolongation of gestation of 7.6 days with metformin vs. placebo (geometric mean ratio 1.39, 95% CI 0.99–1.95; P=0.057), which was statistically non-significant in the primary analysis, though per-protocol analyses were significant; composite maternal, fetal, and neonatal outcomes did not differ (PMID 34551918).
- A planned secondary analysis of the MOMPOD RCT (n=831) found metformin was not associated with lower odds of preterm preeclampsia before 37 weeks (aOR 1.04, 95% CI 0.70–1.56) or before 34 weeks (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 0.73–2.81) compared to placebo in women with early gestational or type 2 diabetes (PMID 39236318).
- A two-country population-based cohort analysis (Scotland and Sweden, n>11,000) found metformin use was not associated with altered risk of preeclampsia (pooled RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.73–1.28) or any hypertensive disorder of pregnancy (pooled RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.79–1.18) in women with diabetes (PMID 39334302).
- A randomized placebo-controlled multicenter trial in 257 women with PCOS found preeclampsia prevalence was numerically higher in the metformin group (7.4%) versus placebo (3.7%), though not statistically significant (P=0.18), and the composite primary endpoint did not differ (PMID 20926533).
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs comparing metformin vs. insulin found a reduced risk of preeclampsia with metformin (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.48–0.95; P=0.02; I²=0%), but meta-analysis of 5 RCTs comparing metformin vs. placebo/control found no significant difference (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.33–2.26; P=0.76; I²=66%) (PMID 29044702).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 35 RCTs (n=8033) found metformin was associated with lower likelihood of preeclampsia versus other treatments (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.50–0.95; I²=55%; P=0.02), though the comparator was predominantly insulin rather than placebo (PMID 33927270).
- A meta-analysis of 24 RCTs in GDM patients found metformin significantly reduced preeclampsia risk compared to insulin (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.48–0.78; P<0.0001) (PMID 38124287).
- A systematic review and network meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found evidence for metformin in preventing preeclampsia was inconsistent with wide confidence intervals, and vitamin D ranked higher than metformin in probabilistic rankings; the authors concluded evidence for metformin remains limited and inconsistent (PMID 42827236).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 RCTs comparing metformin vs. insulin in GDM found the incidence of preeclampsia and hypertensive disorders was similar between groups (PMID 42965204).
- A meta-analysis of 3 RCTs in obese pregnant women without diabetes found metformin did not significantly reduce preeclampsia risk (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.10–1.68; P=0.22; I²=81%), and the authors concluded metformin should not be routinely recommended for preeclampsia prevention in this population (PMID 42924392).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis found the incidence of severe preeclampsia was significantly lower in those who received metformin than those who did not (12.1% vs. 20.7%, aOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.18–0.81), though the review was based on only four empirical studies (PMID 40852348).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded metformin showed greater preventive effects for pregnancy-induced hypertension but was less effective for preeclampsia specifically, and recommended further well-designed trials (PMID 30399639).
- An RCT in 150 GDM patients found preeclampsia was significantly less common in metformin-treated groups compared to insulin alone, though this was an open-label study in a developing country setting (PMID 25467617).

### Miscarriage

#### Literature — Miscarriage

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 8

The preponderance of evidence supports metformin in reducing miscarriage risk in women with PCOS. Multiple studies report lower miscarriage rates with metformin use during pregnancy (PMIDs: 20441061, 11163815, 17162710, 39030462), and a pooled individual participant data analysis across three RCTs found a significant reduction in late miscarriage and preterm birth (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23–0.79; PMID: 30792154). The one RCT showing no difference in miscarriage rates (PMID: 22419702) focused on early miscarriage as a secondary endpoint and did not find a significant effect, while the most recent meta-analysis of RCTs found a trend toward miscarriage reduction when metformin was continued through the first trimester (OR 0.64; PMID: 40473092), with the overall body of evidence favoring continuation of metformin.

**Key findings:**
- A pooled individual participant data analysis of three RCTs found that late miscarriage or preterm delivery occurred in 5% of metformin-treated vs. 10% of placebo-treated women with PCOS (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23–0.79, p=0.004; PMID: 30792154).
- A prospective cohort study found early pregnancy loss rates of 11.6% in the metformin group vs. 36.3% in the control group (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.11–0.42, p<0.0001; PMID: 17162710).
- A pilot study reported first-trimester spontaneous abortion in 10% of metformin-treated women vs. 73% in prior pregnancies without metformin (p<0.002; PMID: 11163815).
- A three-arm study found significant reductions in miscarriage when metformin was continued to 8 or 12 weeks gestation compared to stopping at conception (PMID: 20441061).
- A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs found that continuing metformin through the first trimester was associated with a possible reduction in miscarriage (OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.32–1.25) and that stopping metformin after a positive pregnancy test showed an indication of increased miscarriage (OR 1.46, 95% CI 0.73–2.90; PMID: 40473092).
- A multicenter double-blind RCT found miscarriage rates were low and similar between metformin (15.2%) and placebo (17.9%, P=0.8) groups, providing no significant benefit on miscarriage as a secondary endpoint (PMID: 22419702).
- An observational study of previously infertile PCOS women found a similar miscarriage incidence between metformin and control groups (6.7% vs. 11.1%, P=0.273), though the combined endpoint of miscarriage or preterm delivery was significantly lower in the metformin group (13.3% vs. 24.4%, P=0.046; PMID: 39030462).

### Insulin Resistance

#### Literature — Insulin Resistance

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The evidence is mixed overall. Multiple randomized controlled trials in obese adolescents and HIV lipodystrophy patients demonstrate that metformin significantly reduces insulin resistance biomarkers (fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, insulin AUC) compared to placebo (PMIDs: 18556965, 10904511, 16595599), while one RCT in obese adolescents found no statistically significant advantage of metformin over placebo for HOMA-IR improvement (PMID: 20639355). A well-powered 26-week RCT in adults with type 1 diabetes found no effect of metformin on hepatic insulin resistance as measured by hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (PMID: 41285865), introducing credible contradicting evidence that tempers the otherwise predominantly positive signal.

**Key findings:**
- In a 6-month randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial of obese adolescents with hyperinsulinemia, metformin significantly reduced fasting insulin (19.2 to 11.1 µU/mL, p<0.001) and HOMA-IR (4.95 to 2.6, p<0.001) compared to placebo (PMID: 18556965).
- In a randomized double-blind crossover trial of obese insulin-resistant children and adolescents, metformin had a significantly greater treatment effect over placebo for fasting insulin (-2.2 mU/liter, P=0.011) and body composition measures (PMID: 16595599).
- In a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial of HIV-infected patients with lipodystrophy and abnormal glucose homeostasis, metformin significantly reduced insulin AUC after OGTT compared to placebo (P=0.01) (PMID: 10904511).
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of obese adolescents, HOMA-IR improved similarly in both metformin and placebo groups (73% vs 54% reduction, P=0.048 favoring placebo direction), with most differences not reaching statistical significance, suggesting metformin offered limited additional benefit over placebo plus lifestyle intervention (PMID: 20639355).
- In a 26-week randomized placebo-controlled trial in adults with type 1 diabetes, metformin 1500 mg did not reduce hepatic insulin resistance (endogenous glucose production) compared to placebo (mean difference 0.2 µmol/kgFFM/min, p=0.53) (PMID: 41285865).

### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

#### Literature — Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 12

The evidence on metformin for PCOS is mixed, with clear benefits in some domains and lack of efficacy in others. Metformin demonstrates consistent positive effects on menstrual regularity, hyperandrogenism, and insulin sensitivity (PMIDs 10634377, 10206447, 36763111), and shows some benefit in IVF outcomes such as pregnancy rates and OHSS reduction in certain populations (PMID 16501038, 19757275). However, metformin is inferior to clomiphene for live birth in infertile PCOS women (PMID 17287476), does not consistently improve IVF outcomes (PMIDs 16169430, 21917254), and shows limited reproductive or metabolic benefit in some controlled trials (PMIDs 11994052, 19692630).

**Key findings:**
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial found metformin improved menstrual frequency, reduced free testosterone, and increased insulin sensitivity in PCOS women over 6 months, with 54.8% of subjects showing striking improvements in menstrual abnormalities in the open long-term phase (PMID 10634377).
- In an uncontrolled study, 39 of 43 (91%) previously amenorrheic PCOS women resumed normal menses on metformin, with improvements in testosterone and estradiol independent of weight loss (PMID 10206447).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 randomized placebo-controlled trials found metformin significantly reduced total testosterone levels and free androgen index (FAI) in PCOS women (PMID 36763111).
- A large RCT of 626 infertile PCOS women found live-birth rates of 22.5% for clomiphene vs. 7.2% for metformin alone, concluding clomiphene is superior to metformin for achieving live birth in infertile PCOS women (PMID 17287476).
- A prospective randomized trial found metformin did not improve IVF/ICSI outcomes (fertilization rates, oocyte quality, pregnancy rates) in PCOS patients (PMID 16169430).
- A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial in 8 obese PCOS women found no significant differences in urinary FSH, LH, pregnanediol glucuronide, ovulation rates, serum sex steroids, or glucose disposal between metformin and placebo (PMID 19692630).
- A randomized controlled trial found that in PCOS women with reduced ovarian reserve undergoing IVF, metformin worsened ovarian response to gonadotropins, with significantly fewer retrieved oocytes and higher cancellation rates, leading to early trial termination (PMID 21917254).
- A randomized placebo-controlled double-blind crossover study in clomiphene-resistant PCOS women found no significant differences in spontaneous menstruation, ovulation, or pregnancy rates between metformin and placebo groups (PMID 11994052).
- A randomized placebo-controlled double-blind IVF trial found no difference in fertilization rates or oocyte numbers between metformin and placebo, but metformin significantly improved clinical pregnancy rates (38.5% vs. 16.3%, P=0.023) and reduced severe OHSS incidence (3.8% vs. 20.4%, P=0.023) (PMID 16501038).
- A randomized controlled trial in clomiphene-resistant PCOS women undergoing IVF found metformin significantly improved embryo quality and implantation rate and reduced OHSS and abortion rates, but did not significantly increase overall pregnancy rates (PMID 19757275).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 36 RCTs found metformin was inferior to combined oral contraceptives (COCP) on free androgen index, SHBG, and testosterone, but superior for insulin and triglyceride reduction; combination therapy showed additional benefits over COCP alone for insulin resistance (PMID 37554096).
- A systematic review of 7 RCTs in adolescent PCOS found that while COCs were effective for hyperandrogenic symptoms, insulin-sensitizing regimens including metformin showed superior outcomes in ovulation rates, insulin sensitivity, and body composition, with fewer metabolic side effects (PMID 40491634).

### Infertility

#### Literature — Infertility

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 14

The evidence is mixed: several high-quality RCTs and a multicenter trial show metformin improves pregnancy and live-birth rates in PCOS women, particularly when combined with standard ovulation induction (PMID: 22419702, PMID: 21518135, PMID: 20687400), while other well-powered RCTs demonstrate that metformin is inferior to clomiphene as monotherapy for live birth (PMID: 17287476), worsens ovarian response in PCOS women with reduced reserve (PMID: 21917254), and fails to improve outcomes over placebo in clomiphene-resistant or letrozole-resistant populations (PMID: 11994052, PMID: 30847561, PMID: 41618368). The net picture is context-dependent—metformin shows benefit in certain subpopulations and protocols but not others—making the overall evidence genuinely mixed with no clear universal direction.

**Key findings:**
- In a large RCT of 626 infertile PCOS women, the live-birth rate with metformin monotherapy (7.2%) was significantly lower than with clomiphene (22.5%) or combination therapy (26.8%), establishing clomiphene's superiority for live birth (PMID: 17287476).
- A multicenter double-blind RCT of 320 PCOS women found metformin significantly improved pregnancy rate (53.6% vs. 40.4%) and live-birth rate (41.9% vs. 28.8%) compared to placebo when combined with standard infertility treatment (PMID: 22419702).
- A prospective randomized study in IVF repeaters without PCOS found low-dose metformin significantly improved ongoing pregnancy and implantation rates in women with a high discriminant score for insulin resistance (56% vs. ~20% ongoing pregnancy rate) (PMID: 20687400).
- An RCT of PCOS women with reduced ovarian reserve was stopped early because metformin significantly worsened gonadotropin response, reducing peak estradiol, dominant follicles, retrieved oocytes, and MII oocytes compared to placebo (PMID: 21917254).
- A randomized double-blind crossover trial in clomiphene-resistant PCOS women found no significant difference between metformin and placebo in ovulation induction (5/12 vs. 4/14) or pregnancy rates (3/12 vs. 2/14) (PMID: 11994052).
- A multicenter Nordic RCT found no significant difference in clinical pregnancy rate (39.3% vs. 30.4%) or live-birth rate (37.5% vs. 28.6%) between metformin and placebo among IVF-treated PCOS women, though intention-to-treat analysis including spontaneous pregnancies showed significantly higher overall CPR and LBR with metformin (PMID: 21606131).
- An RCT of 60 PCOS women found both 1000 mg and 1700 mg metformin produced similar ovulation rates (~85-88%) and pregnancy rates (~46-52% per woman), supporting metformin's efficacy for ovulation induction at either dose (PMID: 21518135).
- A randomized controlled trial in letrozole-resistant PCOS women found no significant difference in pregnancy rates among metformin, inositol, and placebo groups, with inositol outperforming metformin in normal-BMI patients (PMID: 30847561).
- A double-blind RCT comparing metformin, myoinositol, Ziziphus jujuba, and placebo found no significant differences in pregnancy rates (metformin: 7, placebo: 4 pregnancies; p > 0.05), though metformin improved some metabolic parameters (PMID: 41618368).
- A narrative review concluded that metformin increases ovulation, pregnancy, and live-birth rates vs. placebo in PCOS, is more effective combined with clomiphene in clomiphene-resistant women, but adding metformin to letrozole is not more effective than letrozole alone, and metformin has an adjunctive role only in long-protocol ART (PMID: 39899277).
- An RCT in insulin-resistant PCOS women undergoing COS found metformin significantly increased mono-ovulatory cycle rates but did not improve ovulation, pregnancy, live-birth, or OHSS rates compared to placebo (PMID: 15958399).

### Heart Failure

#### Literature — Heart Failure

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 8

The evidence on metformin for heart failure is mixed. Several RCTs and a meta-analysis report benefits including improved myocardial efficiency (PMID: 31863557), reduced oxidative stress and left ventricular mass index (PMID: 37816240), improved VE/VCO2 slope (PMID: 22740509), and reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in T2DM+CHF patients (PMID: 39277567), while a systematic review found no consistent effect on left ventricular function (PMID: 32157481) and a placebo-controlled RCT found no influence on cardiac function (PMID: 39829685). The most rigorous evidence thus points in both directions, with functional and mechanistic benefits observed alongside null findings on key clinical cardiac endpoints such as ejection fraction and HF hospitalization.

**Key findings:**
- A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in non-diabetic insulin-resistant HFrEF patients found metformin improved myocardial efficiency (WMI) by ~20% and reduced myocardial oxygen consumption, though changes in ejection fraction and exercise capacity did not differ between groups (PMID: 31863557).
- A randomized controlled trial in nondiabetic HFrEF patients found metformin improved total antioxidant capacity and preserved left ventricular mass index compared to standard of care, but changes in LVEF were not significantly different between groups (PMID: 37816240).
- A double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in non-diabetic insulin-resistant CHF patients found metformin improved insulin resistance and VE/VCO2 slope but did not improve the primary endpoint of peak VO2 (PMID: 22740509).
- A meta-analysis of 15 RCTs in T2DM+CHF patients found metformin was associated with significantly reduced all-cause mortality (RR=0.72) and cardiovascular mortality (RR=0.52), improved NT-proBNP levels, but no significant difference in HF hospitalization risk or LVEF (PMID: 39277567).
- A systematic review of RCTs concluded that metformin did not affect left ventricular function overall, though it could improve myocardial oxygen consumption and reduce NT-proBNP inconsistently between diabetic and nondiabetic patients (PMID: 32157481).
- A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in T2DM+HF patients found metformin improved glycemic control and postprandial GLP-1 secretion but did not influence cardiac function (PMID: 39829685).
- An uncontrolled cohort study in CHF+T2DM patients reported improvements in NYHA functional class, central hemodynamics, quality of life, and reduced CHF decompensations with metformin, with no cases of lactic acidosis observed (PMID: 18429758).

### Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

#### Literature — Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 14

The evidence on metformin as adjunct therapy to insulin in type 1 diabetes is mixed, with no consistent signal on the primary clinical outcome of glycemic control (HbA1c). Several smaller randomized controlled trials and one meta-analysis report HbA1c reductions with metformin (PMIDs 12502670, 14514347, 16978371, 35249270, 35351535, 37599482, 40512873), while larger or longer-duration RCTs and one network meta-analysis find no significant HbA1c benefit (PMIDs 26624824, 18852875, 19496776, 41044631). Across studies, metformin consistently reduces insulin dose and body weight, but at the cost of increased gastrointestinal adverse events, and the glycemic benefit—the primary therapeutic target—remains unproven in the most rigorous evidence.

**Key findings:**
- A large multicenter double-blind RCT (n=140 overweight adolescents) found no significant difference in HbA1c at 26 weeks between metformin and placebo (mean difference 0%, P=0.92), though metformin reduced insulin dose and BMI z-score (PMID 26624824).
- A 12-month double-blind RCT (n=100 adults with poorly controlled T1DM) found no improvement in HbA1c with metformin vs. placebo (difference 0.13%, P=0.422), though insulin dose and body weight were reduced (PMID 18852875).
- A 24-week double-blind RCT (n=24 overweight T1DM patients) found no significant difference in HbA1c between metformin and placebo (P=0.26), but metformin significantly reduced total daily insulin dose and body weight (PMID 19496776).
- A network meta-analysis of adolescent T1DM RCTs (n=764) found that no metformin dose regimen significantly lowered HbA1c versus placebo, though metformin 2.0 g/day reduced BMI, LDL-C, and insulin requirements (PMID 41044631).
- An updated meta-analysis of 29 placebo-controlled RCTs (n=2051) found that metformin significantly reduced HbA1c in adolescents at 3 months (MD=-0.45, 95% CI: -0.79, -0.11) and in adults at 6 months (MD=-0.70, 95% CI: -1.10, -0.30), but no statistical difference was identified at other follow-up endpoints (PMID 40512873).
- A 3-month double-blind RCT (n=27 adolescents) found HbA1c was 0.6% lower in the metformin group than placebo (P<0.05), with reduced insulin dose, but increased mild hypoglycemia (PMID 12502670).
- A 3-month double-blind RCT (n=26 adolescents) found HbA1c decreased significantly with metformin (9.6% to 8.7%, P<0.05) but was unchanged in the placebo group, with improved peripheral insulin sensitivity (PMID 14514347).
- A 16-week crossover RCT (n=15 overweight adults) found HbA1c significantly lower after metformin (7.8%) vs. placebo (8.6%, P<0.005), with reduced fasting glucose and total daily insulin dose (PMID 16978371).
- A 9-month double-blind RCT (n=50 adolescents) found significantly lower HbA1c (P<0.001) and insulin dosage (P=0.04) in the metformin group, along with improvements in lipid profile (PMID 35249270).
- A 24-week RCT (n=80 adolescents with T1DM and microvascular complications) found metformin significantly decreased HbA1c, CRP, UACR, total cholesterol, and CIMT compared with placebo (P<0.001) (PMID 35351535).
- A 3-month clinical trial (n=80 Pakistani adolescents with T1DM) found a significant reduction in HbA1c (P<0.001) and random blood glucose (P=0.006) in the metformin group compared with insulin-only group (PMID 37599482).
- A multicenter RCT in 126 pregnant women with T1DM found no significant difference in total insulin requirement change between metformin and placebo (P=0.193), though prandial insulin increase was attenuated and weight gain was better controlled with metformin (PMID 40916351).
- Gastrointestinal adverse events were significantly more frequent with metformin than placebo in both adolescents (RR=1.74) and adults (RR=3.24) across the meta-analysis (PMID 40512873), and were also reported in the large adolescent RCT (mean difference 36%, P<0.001) (PMID 26624824).

### Sepsis

#### Literature — Sepsis

**Evidence strength:** moderate, supports
**Relevant studies:** 8

The preponderance of evidence supports metformin as beneficial in sepsis. Three independent meta-analyses of observational cohort studies consistently found that preadmission metformin use was associated with significantly reduced mortality in septic patients with diabetes (OR 0.57–0.74) (PMIDs 35002982, 30777119, 32166255), and a retrospective observational study found that metformin administration after septic shock was independently associated with lower 90-day and 365-day mortality (PMIDs 40394230). The single contradicting study (PMID 32146543) was a nationwide cohort that found no significant association, but it is outweighed by the consistent positive signal across multiple meta-analyses and additional observational data, with preclinical mechanistic studies further supporting plausible biological rationale (PMIDs 41401657, 37037116).

**Key findings:**
- A meta-analysis of 11 observational studies (8,195 patients) found preadmission metformin use associated with lower mortality in sepsis patients with diabetes (OR 0.74; 95% CI 0.62–0.88; P < 0.01), without significant increases in serum creatinine or lactic acid (PMID 35002982).
- A meta-analysis of 5 observational cohort studies (1,282 patients) found metformin use associated with significantly lower mortality in septic adult patients with diabetes (OR 0.59; 95% CI 0.43–0.79; P = 0.001) (PMID 30777119).
- A meta-analysis of 8 observational studies (4,144 patients) found premorbid metformin exposure associated with reduced mortality in sepsis (OR 0.57; 95% CI 0.40–0.80) without significant association with hyperlactatemia (PMID 32166255).
- A retrospective observational study of 320 septic shock patients with type 2 diabetes found metformin administration within 48 h was independently associated with decreased 90-day mortality (aHR 0.371; 95% CI 0.153–0.900; P = 0.028) and 365-day mortality (aHR 0.453; 95% CI 0.219–0.937; P = 0.033) (PMID 40394230).
- A nationwide propensity-score-adjusted cohort study of 77,337 diabetic patients found prior metformin therapy was not significantly associated with 30-day mortality after sepsis diagnosis (OR 0.94; 95% CI 0.75–1.17; P = 0.571) (PMID 32146543).
- In a rat LPS-induced endotoxemia model, metformin dose-dependently improved survival and attenuated cardiac, coagulation, and metabolic dysfunction via modulation of VEGF-A/VEGFR1/VEGFR2 signaling (PMID 41401657).
- In macrophage and CLP mouse models, metformin (an AMPK activator) suppressed LPS-induced TGF-β1 production and its downstream Smad2 signaling, thereby inhibiting endotoxin tolerance and restoring pro-inflammatory cytokine responses (PMID 37037116).

### Hyperandrogenism

#### Literature — Hyperandrogenism

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 6

The preponderance of evidence supports metformin as effective for reducing biochemical hyperandrogenism. Two large systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials demonstrate significant reductions in total testosterone and free androgen index with metformin versus placebo in PCOS patients (PMID: 36763111; PMID: 35434762), and a randomized placebo-controlled trial in adolescents with PCOS confirmed significant testosterone reduction and improved menstrual regularity (PMID: 16520442). The single contradicting controlled study (PMID: 7608255) found no benefit of metformin beyond diet alone, and one mixed trial (PMID: 24280743) showed biochemical androgen reductions without clinical improvement, but these are outweighed by the consistent positive findings from higher-level evidence.

**Key findings:**
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 17–18 RCTs found metformin significantly reduced total testosterone levels (SMD: -0.46, 95% CI: -0.89 to -0.02) and regulated free androgen index (FAI) values in women with PCOS compared to control (PMID: 36763111).
- A broader systematic review and meta-analysis of 92 RCTs found significant reductions in total testosterone with metformin versus placebo (SMD: -0.33; 95% CI -0.49 to -0.17, p < 0.0001, moderate grade evidence) in women with PCOS (PMID: 35434762).
- A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 22 adolescents with PCOS showed metformin significantly decreased mean serum testosterone (-38.3 ng/dL vs -0.86 ng/dL with placebo) and increased the relative risk of menses 2.50-fold compared to placebo (PMID: 16520442).

- A double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 24 obese hirsute women on a low-calorie diet found that metformin provided no additional benefit over diet alone in reducing androgens or improving insulin sensitivity, with diet itself significantly reducing androstenedione, non-SHBG-bound testosterone, and fasting insulin (PMID: 7608255).

### Atherosclerosis

#### Literature — Atherosclerosis

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 8

The evidence on metformin for atherosclerosis is mixed. A network meta-analysis of clinical trials found metformin significantly reduced cIMT progression compared to placebo (PMID: 38883606), and a preclinical study demonstrated antiatherosclerotic effects via dampening extramedullary myelopoiesis in normoglycemic mice (PMID: 40758579), but two randomized controlled trials found no significant reduction in mean cIMT with metformin versus placebo in type 2 diabetes (PMID: 26916684) and type 1 diabetes (PMID: 28615149), with only a secondary maximal cIMT measure showing benefit in the latter.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of 412 patients with type 2 diabetes, metformin did not significantly reduce mean carotid IMT compared to placebo (between-group difference 0.012 mm, 95% CI -0.003 to 0.026, p=0.11), though the trial reached only 46% of planned sample size (PMID: 26916684).
- In the REMOVAL trial of 428 adults with type 1 diabetes, metformin did not significantly reduce progression of mean cIMT (-0.005 mm per year, 95% CI -0.012 to 0.002; p=0.1664), although maximal cIMT (a tertiary outcome) was significantly reduced (-0.013 mm per year; p=0.0093) (PMID: 28615149).
- A network meta-analysis of 25 clinical trials (6675 patients) found that metformin was more effective than placebo in slowing cIMT progression (MD=-0.05, 95% CI -0.09 to -0.02), ranking it among the top three antidiabetic drugs for this outcome alongside exenatide and alogliptin (PMID: 38883606).
- In Apoe-/- mice under normoglycemic conditions, metformin decreased atherosclerotic lesion size, reduced plaque macrophages, and lowered circulating atherogenic Ly6-Chi monocytes and neutrophils by suppressing hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell mobilization and extramedullary myelopoiesis (PMID: 40758579).
- Metformin reduced bodyweight (-1.17 kg, p<0.0001) and LDL cholesterol (-0.13 mmol/L, p=0.0117) in the REMOVAL trial, suggesting potential cardiovascular risk factor benefits beyond direct cIMT effects (PMID: 28615149).

### Medulloblastoma

#### Literature — Medulloblastoma

**Evidence strength:** weak, supports
**Relevant studies:** 1

The available evidence supports the potential repurposing of metformin for medulloblastoma based on preclinical data. Metformin inhibited proliferation, metastasis, and induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in Sonic hedgehog (Shh) subgroup medulloblastoma cells in vitro, and suppressed tumor growth in a xenograft mouse model in vivo (PMID: 36278239). The mechanism was linked to AMPK-mediated inhibition of the Shh signaling pathway (PMID: 36278239); no human clinical evidence is available.

**Key findings:**
- Metformin inhibited proliferation and metastasis of the Shh subgroup medulloblastoma cell line (Daoy) in vitro, as assessed by CCK-8, colony formation, scratch-wound, and transwell invasion assays (PMID: 36278239).
- Metformin induced apoptosis and blocked the cell cycle at the G0/G1 phase in Daoy medulloblastoma cells, with effects reversible by Shh pathway activators (PMID: 36278239).
- Metformin decreased mRNA and protein expression levels of the Shh pathway, and this effect was reversed by AMPK siRNA, indicating AMPK-mediated inhibition of the Shh signaling pathway (PMID: 36278239).
- In a xenograft murine model, metformin inhibited medulloblastoma tumor growth in vivo (PMID: 36278239).

### Prostate Cancer

#### Literature — Prostate Cancer

**Evidence strength:** strong, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 11

The highest-quality clinical evidence consistently fails to show that metformin improves prostate cancer outcomes. The phase III MAST trial found no reduction in progression for low-risk prostate cancer on active surveillance (HR 1.09; PMID: 41166665), and the large phase III STAMPEDE trial found no overall survival benefit in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (HR 0.91, p=0.15; PMID: 40639383). A meta-analysis of RCTs confirmed no oncological benefit in unselected patients, with only hypothesis-generating subgroup signals (PMID: 42875601), and observational/chemoprevention data similarly showed no significant association between metformin use and prostate cancer risk or mortality (PMID: 26353947, PMID: 39014063).

**Key findings:**
- Phase III MAST RCT (n=408): metformin did not reduce progression in low-risk prostate cancer on active surveillance (HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.79–1.52, p=0.59); obese patients showed increased pathologic progression on metformin (HR 2.36, p=0.0092). PMID: 41166665
- Phase III STAMPEDE RCT (n=1874): adding metformin to standard of care did not improve overall survival in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.80–1.03, p=0.15). PMID: 40639383
- Systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (n=2415): metformin did not improve overall survival in unselected prostate cancer patients; a high-volume subgroup survival signal from STAMPEDE was hypothesis-generating only (volume interaction p=0.12, non-significant). PMID: 42875601
- REDUCE study (n=540 diabetic men): metformin use was not significantly associated with total (OR 1.19, p=0.50), low-grade (OR 1.01, p=0.96), or high-grade (OR 1.83, p=0.19) prostate cancer diagnosis. PMID: 26353947
- Meta-analysis of real-world studies: metformin was not associated with prostate cancer recurrence (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.81–1.15) or mortality (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.81–1.09), though a reduced incidence signal was observed. PMID: 39014063
- Randomized phase 2 BIMET-1 trial: no difference between metformin plus bicalutamide versus bicalutamide alone in achieving undetectable PSA at 32 weeks; trial stopped early due to predicted inability to achieve primary endpoint. PMID: 35079115
- National population-based retrospective cohort study: longer cumulative metformin use was associated with lower prostate cancer risk (Q4 HR 0.571), but this observational finding is outweighed by null results from controlled trials. PMID: 36974927
- Preclinical study: low-dose metformin inhibited CRPC progression via PDE6D/purine metabolism/cGMP-PKG pathway in mouse models, but clinical translation remains undemonstrated. PMID: 40216151

### Breast Cancer

#### Literature — Breast Cancer

**Evidence strength:** strong, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 12

The preponderance of high-quality clinical evidence contradicts the repurposing of metformin for breast cancer treatment in non-diabetic patients. The large phase III MA.32 randomized controlled trial (n=3,649) found no improvement in invasive disease-free survival or overall survival with metformin versus placebo (PMID: 35608580), and a secondary analysis of the same trial showed no reduction in new primary cancer risk (PMID: 37695982). A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found no significant difference in pathological complete response rates between metformin and control groups (PMID: 41161296), and a phase II RCT in metastatic breast cancer showed no benefit in progression-free or overall survival (PMID: 31472446). While some biomarker and smaller trials show biological activity or subgroup signals, these do not offset the consistent null findings from controlled clinical outcome studies.

**Key findings:**
- In the MA.32 phase III RCT (n=3,649), metformin versus placebo did not significantly improve invasive disease-free survival (HR 1.01; 95% CI 0.84-1.21) or overall survival (HR 1.10; 95% CI 0.86-1.41) in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer patients (PMID: 35608580).
- A secondary analysis of MA.32 found that metformin did not reduce the risk of new primary cancer development; 102 patients in the metformin arm versus 82 in the placebo arm developed new invasive cancers (HR 1.25; 95% CI 0.94-1.68; P=0.13) (PMID: 37695982).
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found no statistically significant difference in pathological complete response rates between metformin and control groups in neoadjuvant breast cancer treatment (RR=1.21; 95% CI 0.85-1.71; P=0.28) (PMID: 41161296).
- A phase II randomized trial in metastatic breast cancer found no significant benefit of metformin added to chemotherapy; mean PFS was 5.4 vs 6.3 months and mean OS was 20.2 vs 24.2 months (metformin vs placebo), with HRs trending against metformin (PMID: 31472446).
- A phase 2 RCT (McBETH, n=242) evaluating metformin added to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in HER2-positive and triple-negative breast cancer found no significant improvement in pathological complete response rates (43.7% vs 41.7%; P=0.75) (PMID: 41348549).
- A preoperative window-of-opportunity randomized trial found that metformin produced significant biological effects including upregulation of pAMPK, downregulation of pAkt, and reduction in Ki67, suggesting cytostatic mechanisms in vivo, but these were biomarker endpoints only (PMID: 25682077).
- A presurgical randomized trial found that metformin significantly decreased Ki67 only in subgroups with insulin resistance markers (HOMA >2.8) or HER2-positive tumors, indicating heterogeneous and context-dependent biological effects at the biomarker level (PMID: 25253174).
- A small RCT (n=70, METNEO study) reported significantly improved pathological complete response and clinical response rates with metformin added to neoadjuvant AC-T chemotherapy, along with upregulated death receptor expression and reduced CD133+ cancer stem cells, but this single small trial is outweighed by larger contradicting evidence (PMID: 39113190).
- A scoping review of 40 RCTs noted that most studies did not present results by breast cancer phenotype, and that limited subgroup evidence suggested potential benefit only in HER2+ patients, a finding requiring further investigation (PMID: 40841629).

### Endothelial Dysfunction

#### Literature — Endothelial Dysfunction

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 8

The preponderance of controlled trial evidence supports metformin's efficacy in improving endothelial function across multiple patient populations. Five randomized placebo-controlled trials demonstrated significant improvements in endothelium-dependent vasodilation with metformin in patients with type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and high-risk relatives of diabetic patients (PMIDs: 11300445, 16115299, 15606381, 16644641, 23140261). The two mixed-verdict sources reflect nuance rather than contradiction: one RCT found benefit only in PCOS patients with baseline endothelial dysfunction (PMID: 31806099), and a scoping review noted that studies with active comparators showed weaker effects than placebo-controlled studies, while still identifying a trend toward improvement across most biomarkers (PMID: 41057192).

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of 44 type 2 diabetes patients, metformin significantly improved acetylcholine-stimulated forearm blood flow (p=0.0027) with no effect on endothelium-independent responses, and insulin resistance was the sole predictor of endothelium-dependent flow after treatment (PMID: 11300445).
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of 65 metabolic syndrome patients, metformin improved flow-mediated dilation from 7.4% to 12.4% vs. no change with placebo (p=0.0016), with a significant correlation between improvement in insulin resistance and endothelial function (PMID: 16115299).
- In a double-blind RCT of 390 insulin-treated type 2 diabetes patients, metformin significantly decreased von Willebrand factor by 6% (p=0.0007), soluble VCAM-1 by 4% (p=0.0002), E-selectin by 6% (p=0.008), and PAI-1 by 20% (p=0.0001) compared to placebo, independent of glycemic control changes (PMID: 15606381).
- In a double-blind RCT of 31 first-degree relatives of type 2 diabetic patients with metabolic syndrome, metformin improved endothelium-dependent forearm blood flow responses to acetylcholine without affecting endothelium-independent responses, and independently of antihyperglycemic effects (PMID: 16644641).
- A single dose of metformin (500 mg) prevented the decline in brachial artery flow-mediated dilation caused by acute glucose load in hypertensive patients and inhibited glucose load-induced declines in antioxidant markers SOD and T-AOC (PMID: 23140261).
- In an RCT of 42 PCOS patients, metformin showed no overall improvement in endothelial function vs. no treatment, but significantly improved endothelial function in the subgroup with abnormal baseline endothelial function (1.3±0.3 vs. 1.7±0.3; p<0.001), while those with normal baseline showed no change (PMID: 31806099).
- A scoping review of 56 studies (4620 participants) found metformin showed a trend toward improvement for 7 of 19 endothelial biomarkers, but studies with active comparators reported significant differences in only 20% of cases vs. 83.3% in placebo-controlled or pre-post studies, highlighting the importance of study design on observed effect size (PMID: 41057192).

WARNING Metformin Hydrochloride Lactic acidosis: Lactic acidosis is a rare, but serious, metabolic complication that can occur due to metformin accumulation during treatment with Glipizide and Metformin HCl Tablets; when it occurs, it is fatal in approximately 50% of cases. Lactic acidosis may also occur in association with a number of pathophysiologic conditions, including diabetes mellitus, and whenever there is significant tissue hypoperfusion and hypoxemia. Lactic acidosis is characterized by elevated blood lactate levels (>5 mmol/L), decreased blood pH, electrolyte disturbances with an increased anion gap, and an increased lactate/pyruvate ratio. When metformin is implicated as the cause of lactic acidosis, metformin plasma levels >5 μg/mL are generally found. The reported incidence of lactic acidosis in patients receiving Metformin hydrochloride is very low (approximately 0.03 cases/1000 patient-years, with approximately 0.015 fatal cases/1000 patient-years). In more than 20,000 patient-years exposure to metformin in clinical trials, there were no reports of lactic acidosis.
