## Findings by Disease

### Attention Deficit-hyperactivity Disorder

#### Literature — Attention Deficit-hyperactivity Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 12

The preponderance of controlled trial evidence supports bupropion as an effective treatment for ADHD across age groups and formulations. Multiple randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials in both adults (PMIDs 11156812, 15820237, 25325205) and children (PMID 8885585) demonstrated statistically significant superiority of bupropion over placebo, and active-comparator trials found bupropion comparable in efficacy to methylphenidate (PMIDs 7775360, 22806822). The single contradicting controlled trial (PMID 16102908) involved a highly specific and confounded population of methadone-maintained patients with high comorbid cocaine dependence, limiting its generalizability, and the one mixed-verdict trial (PMID 11791949) showed numerically favorable response rates for bupropion but was underpowered to reach statistical significance.

**Key findings:**
- In a 6-week double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in adults, bupropion SR produced a 42% reduction in ADHD symptoms vs. 24% for placebo, with 76% of bupropion-treated patients responding vs. 37% on placebo (PMID 11156812).
- A multisite, 8-week placebo-controlled RCT of bupropion XL in 162 adults with ADHD found 53% responders on bupropion vs. 31% on placebo (p=.004), with an effect size of 0.6 on the ADHD Rating Scale (PMID 15820237).
- A 6-week double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in 42 adults found a significant difference in CAARS scores favoring bupropion 150 mg/day over placebo after 6 weeks (PMID 25325205).
- A multisite, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 109 children aged 6–12 years found significant treatment effects for bupropion over placebo on teacher and parent Conners ratings and cognitive performance measures (PMID 8885585).
- A double-blind crossover trial in 15 children/adolescents found bupropion and methylphenidate produced equivalent and significant improvement on teacher ratings, CGI, and cognitive measures, with neither drug superior to the other (PMID 7775360).
- A 6-week randomized double-blind study in 44 children/adolescents found no significant difference between bupropion and methylphenidate on Parent and Teacher ADHD-RS-IV scores, with 90% response rates on the parent scale in both groups (PMID 22806822).
- A randomized double-blind three-arm trial in 98 methadone-maintained adults with ADHD found no significant differences between bupropion SR, methylphenidate SR, and placebo in reducing ADHD symptoms, with a high placebo response rate of 46% (PMID 16102908).
- A randomized double-blind parallel trial comparing bupropion SR, methylphenidate, and placebo in 30 adults showed response rates of 64%, 50%, and 27% respectively, but the difference between active treatments and placebo did not reach statistical significance (p=0.14), likely due to small sample size (PMID 11791949).
- An open trial of bupropion in 19 adults with ADHD found 14 patients experienced moderate to marked benefit, with 10 choosing to continue bupropion over their prior medication (PMID 2115746).
- A 12-week single-blind trial of bupropion in 11 adults with comorbid cocaine dependence and ADHD found significant reductions in attention difficulties, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, with improvements comparable to a historical methylphenidate cohort (PMID 11916368).
- A 5-week open-label bupropion trial in 13 adolescent boys with ADHD, conduct disorder, and substance use disorders showed significant declines in Conners Hyperactivity Index scores and CGI-Severity ratings (PMID 9847499).

### Cocaine Use Disorder

#### Literature — Cocaine Use Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 7

The evidence on bupropion for cocaine use disorder is mixed, with some controlled and observational studies suggesting benefit and others showing no effect. A systematic review and meta-analysis found a statistically significant increase in abstinence with bupropion (RR 1.63, 95% CI 1.02–2.59), and a large retrospective cohort study found bupropion associated with higher CUD remission rates (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.27–1.94) (PMIDs 31183685, 37703770); however, a randomized controlled trial found no overall benefit of bupropion SR over placebo on cocaine use outcomes (PMID 36920397), and a human laboratory study found that bupropion alone or combined with naltrexone did not reduce cocaine self-administration (PMID 36805862). The net picture is genuinely mixed, with comparably credible evidence pointing in both directions.

**Key findings:**
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 2 RCTs found bupropion significantly increased cocaine abstinence compared to placebo (RR 1.63, 95% CI 1.02–2.59), though the strength of evidence was rated low (PMID 31183685).
- A large nationwide retrospective cohort study (n=161,544) found bupropion associated with higher CUD remission rates compared to other antidepressants (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.27–1.94), with similar findings in patients with co-occurring nicotine dependence (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.11–1.71) (PMID 37703770).
- A 26-week double-blind placebo-controlled RCT found no significant overall difference in cocaine abstinence between bupropion SR and placebo when combined with a financial incentive program, though a subgroup analysis suggested benefit for participants in the abstinence initiation group (66.7% vs. 30.0% abstinent at end of study, P=.04) (PMID 36920397).
- A randomized human laboratory study found that bupropion (up to 400 mg/day), alone or combined with naltrexone, did not significantly decrease cocaine self-administration on either a progressive ratio or relapse procedure in participants with cocaine use disorder (PMID 36805862).
- A retrospective health claims analysis found bupropion was associated with increased remission odds for amphetamine use disorder (OR 2.1, 99% CI 1.09–3.89) but not specifically for cocaine use disorder (PMID 30815171).
- A preclinical study found that high-dose systemic bupropion (60 mg/kg) attenuated cocaine self-administration in male and female rats during extended-access sessions without altering locomotor activity or food intake (PMID 39881654).

### Alcohol Dependence

#### Literature — Alcohol Dependence

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 1

The single available RCT (PMID: 40487775) yields a mixed verdict for bupropion monotherapy in alcohol use disorder: the Placebo + Bupropion arm was not formally tested against placebo due to the sequential hierarchical statistical design being stopped after the Varenicline + Bupropion vs. Varenicline + Placebo comparison was non-significant, leaving bupropion's independent efficacy unestablished. While the combination of Varenicline + Bupropion did reduce alcohol consumption versus placebo (PMID: 40487775), bupropion's individual contribution could not be isolated, and bupropion did provide a clinically meaningful reduction in varenicline-induced nausea (PMID: 40487775).

**Key findings:**
- In the mITT analysis, Varenicline + Bupropion reduced B-PEth (Cohen's d = 0.39, p = 0.004) and %HDD (d = 0.31, p = 0.008) versus Placebo + Placebo, but the difference between Varenicline + Bupropion and Varenicline + Placebo was non-significant (B-PEth: d = 0.022, p = 0.97; %HDD: d = 0.027, p = 0.76), precluding formal testing of the Placebo + Bupropion arm (PMID: 40487775).
- Because the sequential hierarchical statistical method was stopped after the Varenicline + Bupropion vs. Varenicline + Placebo comparison failed significance, the independent efficacy of bupropion monotherapy versus placebo for AUD was not formally evaluated in this trial (PMID: 40487775).
- Bupropion reduced varenicline-induced nausea incidence (36/100 in Varenicline + Bupropion vs. 49/96 in Varenicline + Placebo, p = 0.048) and shortened its median duration (16.5 vs. 45 days, p = 0.010), suggesting a beneficial safety role for bupropion as an adjunct (PMID: 40487775).
- Per-protocol analyses showed the largest effect sizes for Varenicline + Bupropion (d = 0.43 for B-PEth; d = 0.41 for %HDD) compared with placebo, suggesting a potential additive benefit of bupropion when adherence is high, though this remains exploratory (PMID: 40487775).

### Bipolar Disorder

#### Literature — Bipolar Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 7

The evidence on bupropion for bipolar disorder is mixed: controlled and observational studies show bupropion has antidepressant efficacy comparable to active comparators in bipolar depression and a lower risk of mood switching than desipramine or venlafaxine (PMIDs 7929019, 16880481, 16449476), while one large observational study found bupropion monotherapy associated with significantly lower psychiatric hospitalization risk than lithium (PMID 29920885). However, uncontrolled case series raise concerns about bupropion precipitating manic or hypomanic episodes at rates similar to other antidepressants (PMID 1487473), and the controlled trials show that threshold mood switches still occur with bupropion and that sustained antidepressant response in continuation treatment is modest (PMID 16449476), leaving the net benefit-risk profile uncertain.

**Key findings:**
- In a double-blind trial, bupropion and desipramine showed similar antidepressant efficacy in bipolar depression, but mania/hypomania occurred in only 1/9 bupropion-treated patients versus 5/10 desipramine-treated patients (PMID 7929019).
- In a 10-week randomized trial, bupropion, sertraline, and venlafaxine showed similar acute response rates (49–53%) and remission rates (34–41%), but venlafaxine carried a significantly higher risk of mood switching than bupropion or sertraline (PMID 16880481).
- Across acute and continuation randomized trials, venlafaxine had the highest ratio of threshold switches to subthreshold hypomanias, while bupropion had the lowest; however, sustained antidepressant response without a threshold switch occurred in only 16.2% of acute antidepressant trials overall (PMID 16449476).
- In a single-blind study, bupropion SR added to mood stabilizers produced a significant reduction in depressive symptoms (59% response rate) with no cases of affective switch (PMID 12180276).
- An open trial found that most bipolar/schizoaffective patients maintained on bupropion alone or with low-dose adjuncts for ≥1 year showed little or no relapse (PMID 2856918).

### Neuropathic Pain

#### Literature — Neuropathic Pain

**Evidence strength:** moderate, supports
**Relevant studies:** 3

The evidence supports repurposing bupropion for neuropathic pain. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 41 nondepressed patients demonstrated that bupropion SR (150–300 mg daily) significantly reduced average pain scores compared to placebo and improved quality of life (PMID: 11706096). Preclinical mechanistic data further show that intrathecal bupropion produces dose-dependent antihyperalgesic effects in a rat spinal nerve ligation model, mediated through increased spinal noradrenaline and dopamine acting on α2-adrenoceptors and D2 receptors (PMID: 25427287), and a narrative review identifies bupropion as showing promise for neuropathic pain based on its noradrenaline and dopamine reuptake inhibition (PMID: 20185402).

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of 41 nondepressed patients, bupropion SR reduced mean average pain scores by 1.7 points (from 5.7 to 4.0, p < 0.001) compared to no change with placebo (PMID: 11706096).
- 73% of patients receiving bupropion SR reported neuropathic pain as improved or much improved, and one patient became pain-free (PMID: 11706096).
- Pain relief with bupropion SR was significant as early as week 2 (p < 0.05) and continued through weeks 3–6 (p < 0.001), with a significant decrease in pain interference with quality of life (PMID: 11706096).
- Side effects of bupropion SR (dry mouth, insomnia, headache, gastrointestinal upset, tremor, constipation, dizziness) were not dose-limiting (PMID: 11706096).
- Intrathecal bupropion produced a dose-dependent antihyperalgesic effect (3, 10, 30, and 100 µg, p < 0.001) in a rat spinal nerve ligation model of neuropathic pain (PMID: 25427287).
- The antihyperalgesic effect of intrathecal bupropion was reversed by the α2-adrenoceptor antagonist idazoxan and the D2 receptor antagonist sulpiride, implicating spinal noradrenaline and dopamine pathways (PMID: 25427287).
- In vivo microdialysis confirmed that intrathecal bupropion increased noradrenaline and dopamine concentrations in the spinal dorsal horn (p < 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively) (PMID: 25427287).
- Bupropion, as a specific inhibitor of neuronal noradrenaline reuptake and weak inhibitor of dopamine reuptake, shows promise in the treatment of neuropathic pain (PMID: 20185402).

### Narcolepsy

#### Literature — Narcolepsy

**Evidence strength:** weak, supports
**Relevant studies:** 1

A single case report (PMID: 9614600) describes a 46-year-old narcoleptic woman treated with bupropion 100 mg three times daily, which normalized her subjective and objective sleepiness (mean sleep latency improved from 1.8 to 9.1 minutes), REM-sleep propensity, and mood; discontinuation led to reversion of all parameters. The evidence supports repurposing bupropion for narcolepsy, though it is based solely on a single case report and the abstract itself calls for further controlled investigation.

**Key findings:**
- Bupropion 100 mg t.i.d. normalized objective sleepiness in a narcoleptic patient, increasing mean sleep latency from 1.8 minutes to 9.1 minutes on the multiple sleep latency test (PMID: 9614600).
- Bupropion eliminated sleep onset REM-sleep periods during the five-nap MSLT and normalized REM-sleep propensity in a narcoleptic patient (PMID: 9614600).
- Upon discontinuation of bupropion, sleepiness and REM-sleep propensity reverted to pretreatment levels, suggesting a direct drug effect (PMID: 9614600).
- The authors attributed the anti-narcoleptic effects to bupropion's blockade of dopamine or norepinephrine reuptake and called for open-label and placebo-controlled trials (PMID: 9614600).

### Parkinson Disease

#### Literature — Parkinson Disease

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 1

The single available study on bupropion in Parkinson's disease yielded mixed results: approximately half of patients showed meaningful motor improvement, but antidepressant efficacy was limited and side effects were frequent and dose-limiting (PMID: 6431314). The paper-level verdict for this study is itself classified as mixed, reflecting the inconsistent outcomes across motor and mood endpoints (PMID: 6431314). Overall, the evidence base is too limited and internally inconsistent to support a clear positive or negative conclusion.

**Key findings:**
- In 20 patients with idiopathic Parkinson's disease, parkinsonism lessened by at least 30% on disability scales in half the patients treated with bupropion (PMID: 6431314).
- Depression, present in 12 of 20 patients, was alleviated in only 5, indicating limited antidepressant efficacy in this population (PMID: 6431314).
- Side effects were frequent and dose-limiting in five patients, raising tolerability concerns for bupropion use in Parkinson's disease (PMID: 6431314).
- The authors concluded that bupropion is only mildly efficacious in Parkinson's disease, reflecting the inconsistent benefit observed across endpoints (PMID: 6431314).

### Binge Eating Disorder

#### Literature — Binge Eating Disorder

**Evidence strength:** moderate, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 1

A single randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (PMID: 23656848) evaluated bupropion 300 mg/day for 8 weeks in overweight/obese women with BED and found no significant difference between bupropion and placebo on binge-eating frequency, food craving, eating disorder psychopathology, or depression. The evidence contradicts repurposing bupropion as a stand-alone treatment for binge eating disorder, though bupropion did produce modestly greater weight loss than placebo (PMID: 23656848).

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 61 overweight/obese women with DSM-IV-TR BED, bupropion 300 mg/day for 8 weeks did not significantly differ from placebo on binge-eating frequency, the primary outcome (PMID: 23656848).
- Bupropion produced significantly greater weight loss compared to placebo (1.8% vs 0.6% BMI loss; F=10.57, P=.002), but this effect was described as quite modest (PMID: 23656848).
- No significant differences between bupropion and placebo were found on secondary outcomes including food craving, eating disorder psychopathology, or depression levels (PMID: 23656848).
- The authors concluded that findings do not support bupropion as a stand-alone treatment for BED (PMID: 23656848).

### Post-traumatic Stress Disorder

#### Literature — Post-traumatic Stress Disorder

**Evidence strength:** moderate, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 3

The preponderance of evidence contradicts repurposing bupropion for PTSD. A placebo-controlled trial found no significant between-group difference for bupropion SR versus placebo in PTSD symptom reduction (PMID: 17414245), and a systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that bupropion failed to differentiate from placebo in controlled studies (PMID: 27126398). The only supporting evidence comes from a small case series of four patients with comorbid PTSD and methamphetamine use disorder, which is insufficient to offset the controlled negative evidence (PMID: 39175424).

**Key findings:**
- A placebo-controlled 8-week RCT of bupropion SR in 30 PTSD patients found no significant between-group differences in PTSD symptom reduction compared to placebo (PMID: 17414245).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis found that bupropion failed to differentiate from placebo in randomized controlled trials for PTSD, placing it among medications without demonstrated efficacy (PMID: 27126398).
- A case series of four patients with comorbid PTSD and methamphetamine use disorder reported greater reductions in PTSD checklist scores and lower relapse rates with bupropion-containing regimens compared to serotonergic agents alone, but the very small sample and uncontrolled design limit the weight of this finding (PMID: 39175424).
- Trauma-focused psychotherapies and select agents such as sertraline and venlafaxine outperformed bupropion in head-to-head comparisons within the meta-analytic framework (PMID: 27126398).

### Low Back Pain

**Relevant studies:** 1

The evidence contradicts repurposing bupropion for non-neuropathic chronic low back pain.

**Key findings:**

- The authors noted that antidepressants with both noradrenergic and serotonergic effects may have greater efficacy in chronic low back pain than bupropion, which inhibits reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine (PMID: 16202958).

Forfivo XL (effective 20251106):
WARNING: SUICIDAL THOUGHTS AND BEHAVIORS SUICIDALITY AND ANTIDEPRESSANT DRUGS Antidepressants increased the risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults in short‑term trials. These trials did not show an increase in the risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior with antidepressant use in subjects over age 24; there was a reduction in risk with antidepressant use in subjects aged 65 and older [see Warnings and Precautions (5.1)] . In patients of all ages who are started on antidepressant therapy, monitor closely for worsening and for emergence of suicidal thoughts and behaviors. Advise families and caregivers of the need for close observation and communication with the prescriber. FORFIVO XL is not approved for use in pediatric patients [see Warnings and Precautions ( 5.1 )] .
