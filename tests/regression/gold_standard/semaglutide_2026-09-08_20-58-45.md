## Drug Safety

_Drug-wide safety signal — applies to the drug generally, not to any single candidate indication below._

**Regulatory label:** FDA label boxed-warning text (5 product labels, LLM-summarized — see appendix for verbatim text):
Across all five labels, the shared core boxed warning is that semaglutide causes dose-dependent and treatment-duration-dependent thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents at clinically relevant exposures; whether this risk extends to humans, including medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), has not been determined. All labels contraindicate semaglutide in patients with a personal or family history of MTC or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2), and all direct clinicians to counsel patients on MTC risk and symptoms such as a neck mass, dysphagia, dyspnea, or persistent hoarseness. All labels except WEGOVY's abbreviated summary statement also explicitly note that routine monitoring of serum calcitonin or thyroid ultrasound is of uncertain value for early MTC detection. One substantive difference in product scope exists: the first label (OZEMPIC) also names RYBELSUS (the oral tablet formulation of semaglutide) throughout its boxed warning text, applying the same contraindications and counseling requirements to that product as well, whereas the remaining four labels restrict their warnings solely to the injectable product each label covers (Ozempic or Wegovy).

## Findings by Disease

### Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Steatohepatitis

#### Literature — Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Steatohepatitis

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 9

Multiple randomized controlled trials provide evidence for semaglutide in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH/NASH). The pivotal phase 3 trial (PMID: 40305708) demonstrated that semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly significantly improved liver histology, with 62.9% of patients achieving steatohepatitis resolution without fibrosis worsening versus 34.3% on placebo, and 36.8% achieving fibrosis reduction versus 22.4% on placebo. However, earlier trials showed mixed results: the phase 2 trial in patients with NASH and compensated cirrhosis (PMID: 36934740) failed to meet its primary endpoint for fibrosis improvement, and a trial in NAFLD (PMID: 34570916) showed significant liver fat reduction but no significant improvement in liver stiffness, indicating that the evidence supports semaglutide for MASH resolution and fibrosis improvement in non-cirrhotic patients but is less clear in advanced (cirrhotic) disease.

**Key findings:**
- In a phase 3 RCT of 800 patients with biopsy-defined MASH and fibrosis stage 2 or 3, semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly achieved steatohepatitis resolution without fibrosis worsening in 62.9% vs 34.3% placebo (P<0.001), and fibrosis reduction without steatohepatitis worsening in 36.8% vs 22.4% placebo (P<0.001) at week 72 (PMID: 40305708).
- Combined resolution of steatohepatitis and reduction in liver fibrosis occurred in 32.7% of semaglutide-treated patients vs 16.1% of placebo patients in the phase 3 trial (P<0.001) (PMID: 40305708).
- Mean body weight change was -10.5% with semaglutide vs -2.0% with placebo in the phase 3 MASH trial (P<0.001) (PMID: 40305708).
- In a phase 2 RCT of 320 patients with biopsy-confirmed NASH (F1-F3), semaglutide 0.4 mg once daily achieved NASH resolution without fibrosis worsening in 59% vs 17% placebo (P<0.001), but did not significantly improve fibrosis stage (43% vs 33%, P=0.48) (PMID: 33185364).
- In a phase 2 RCT of patients with NASH-related compensated cirrhosis, semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly did not significantly improve fibrosis by one or more stages without NASH worsening (11% vs 29% placebo, p=0.087), failing to meet its primary endpoint (PMID: 36934740).
- In a randomized trial of 67 subjects with NAFLD, semaglutide 0.4 mg once daily significantly reduced liver steatosis (estimated treatment ratio 0.47 at week 48, P<0.0001) but did not significantly improve liver stiffness by MRE (estimated treatment ratio 0.96, P=0.2798) (PMID: 34570916).
- Semaglutide was associated with significant improvements in physical component summary HRQoL scores (ETD 4.26; 95% CI 1.96-6.55; P=0.0003) in patients with biopsy-proven NASH and fibrosis at 72 weeks (PMID: 37328931).
- In a phase 2 open-label trial, semaglutide 2.4 mg monotherapy in NASH patients (F2-F3) produced a mean absolute MRI-PDFF reduction of -8.0%, and combinations with cilofexor and/or firsocostat produced greater reductions (-9.8 to -11.0%) despite similar weight loss (PMID: 35439567).
- In a phase 2 RCT of 698 patients with MASH and clinically significant fibrosis (F2-F4c), semaglutide 2.4 mg monotherapy achieved fibrosis improvement without MASH worsening in 30% vs 16% placebo (nominally significant; EDP 14.05, 95% CI 1.88-26.23; p=0.024) at week 52 (PMID: 42456707).
- A post hoc analysis of three RCTs in MASLD/MASH patients showed overall mean weight change of -11.1 kg (-11.7%) with semaglutide vs -0.7 kg (-0.6%) with placebo, with similar treatment differences regardless of type 2 diabetes status (PMID: 39609879).
- In a 24-week open-label trial in Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes, semaglutide significantly elevated the liver-to-spleen ratio (indicating reduced liver fat) compared to dulaglutide, which did not achieve this effect (PMID: 37646192).

At the Phase 3 level, NCT04822181 — a registrational study titled 'Research Study on Whether Semaglutide Works in People With Non-alcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH)' — is currently active and not recruiting, representing the lead late-stage program for this indication.

### Heart Failure

#### Literature — Heart Failure

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 12

Multiple large randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials demonstrate that semaglutide significantly improves outcomes in patients with heart failure, particularly heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). In the STEP-HFpEF and STEP-HFpEF DM trials, semaglutide improved heart failure-related symptoms (KCCQ-CSS), body weight, exercise function, and inflammatory markers compared to placebo in patients with obesity-related HFpEF with and without type 2 diabetes (PMID: 37622681, 38587233, 38599221). A prespecified analysis of the SELECT trial showed semaglutide reduced MACE and composite heart failure endpoints in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and a history of heart failure, regardless of heart failure subtype (PMID: 39181597), and a pooled analysis across SELECT, FLOW, STEP-HFpEF, and STEP-HFpEF DM confirmed semaglutide reduced the risk of cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure events in patients with HFpEF (PMID: 39222642). The evidence consistently supports the use of semaglutide to treat heart failure, particularly HFpEF in the context of obesity.

**Key findings:**
- In the STEP-HFpEF trial (529 patients with HFpEF and obesity, no diabetes), semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly for 52 weeks improved KCCQ-CSS by a mean of 16.6 points vs 8.7 points with placebo (estimated difference 7.8 points; P<0.001) and reduced body weight by 13.3% vs 2.6% (P<0.001), with improvements in 6-minute walk distance and hierarchical composite endpoint (PMID: 37622681).
- In the STEP-HFpEF DM trial (616 patients with HFpEF, obesity, and type 2 diabetes), semaglutide improved KCCQ-CSS by 13.7 points vs 6.4 points with placebo (estimated difference 7.3 points; P<0.001) and reduced body weight by 9.8% vs 3.4% (P<0.001), with significant improvements in 6-minute walk distance, hierarchical composite endpoint, and CRP (PMID: 38587233).
- In a prespecified pooled analysis of STEP-HFpEF and STEP-HFpEF DM (n=1145), semaglutide improved KCCQ-CSS (mean between-group difference 7.5 points; P<0.0001), reduced body weight (-8.4%; P<0.0001), improved 6-minute walk distance (17.1 m; P<0.0001), and reduced CRP (treatment ratio 0.64; P<0.0001) vs placebo, with effects consistent across subgroups (PMID: 38599221).
- In a prespecified analysis of the SELECT trial (4286 patients with a history of heart failure), semaglutide reduced MACE (HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.60-0.87), the composite heart failure endpoint of cardiovascular death or hospitalization/urgent visit for heart failure (HR 0.79; 95% CI 0.64-0.98), cardiovascular death (HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.59-0.97), and all-cause death (HR 0.81; 95% CI 0.66-1.00) vs placebo, with benefits seen in both HFpEF and HFrEF subtypes (PMID: 39181597).
- In a pooled post-hoc analysis of SELECT, FLOW, STEP-HFpEF, and STEP-HFpEF DM (3743 participants with HFpEF), semaglutide reduced the composite of cardiovascular death or heart failure events (HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.53-0.89; P=0.0045) and worsening heart failure events alone (HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.41-0.82; P=0.0019) vs placebo (PMID: 39222642).
- In the FLOW trial (3533 participants with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease), semaglutide 1 mg once weekly reduced the composite of heart failure events or cardiovascular death (HR 0.73; 95% CI 0.62-0.87; P=0.0005), heart failure events alone (HR 0.73; 95% CI 0.58-0.92; P=0.0068), and cardiovascular death alone (HR 0.71; 95% CI 0.56-0.89; P=0.0036) vs placebo, with consistent effects regardless of baseline heart failure history (PMID: 39217553).
- In an echocardiography substudy of the STEP-HFpEF Program (491 of 1145 participants), semaglutide attenuated progression of left atrial remodeling (estimated mean difference in LA volume -6.13 mL; P=0.0013), reduced right ventricular enlargement, and improved diastolic function parameters (E-wave velocity, E/A ratio, E/e' average) vs placebo, suggesting disease-modifying effects in obesity-related HFpEF (PMID: 39217567).

### Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

#### Literature — Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

**Evidence strength:** strong, supports
**Relevant studies:** 6

Multiple randomized controlled trials demonstrate that semaglutide, as adjunct therapy to insulin (including automated insulin delivery systems), improves glycemic control, reduces body weight, and lowers insulin requirements in adults with type 1 diabetes. The ADJUST-T1D trial showed that significantly more patients on semaglutide achieved the composite endpoint of time-in-range >70%, time-below-range <4%, and ≥5% weight reduction compared to placebo (PMID: 40550013), and a crossover RCT confirmed semaglutide increased time in target glucose range without increasing hypoglycemia (PMID: 39794615). The evidence supports repurposing semaglutide for type 1 diabetes, though safety considerations including gastrointestinal side effects and rare euglycemic ketosis require monitoring (PMID: 39794615, PMID: 41175929).

**Key findings:**
- In the ADJUST-T1D trial (26-week, double-blind RCT, n=72 adults with T1D and obesity on automated insulin delivery), 36% of semaglutide-treated patients vs. 0% of placebo patients achieved the composite endpoint of CGM time-in-range >70%, time-below-range <4%, and ≥5% weight reduction (p<0.001) (PMID: 40550013).
- Semaglutide reduced HbA1c by 0.3 percentage points, increased time-in-range by 8.8 percentage points, and reduced body weight by 8.8 kg compared to placebo over 26 weeks in adults with T1D and obesity (PMID: 40550013).
- In a double-blind, randomized crossover trial (n=28, 24 completed), semaglutide as adjunct to automated insulin delivery increased time in target glucose range (3.9–10.0 mmol/L) by a mean 4.8 percentage points vs. placebo (P=0.006) without increasing time below 3.9 mmol/L (PMID: 39794615).
- Two episodes of recurrent euglycemic ketosis without acidosis occurred during semaglutide use in the crossover trial, though no diabetic ketoacidosis or severe hypoglycemia was observed (PMID: 39794615).
- Post hoc analysis of ADJUST-T1D found semaglutide produced a significant 22.6% reduction in total daily insulin dose over 26 weeks, driven more by bolus (-30.5%) than basal (-15.6%) insulin reductions, with early effects largely independent of weight loss (PMID: 41429002).
- In a post hoc analysis of a crossover RCT, daily total and bolus insulin and carbohydrate input were significantly reduced by day 7 of semaglutide initiation in T1D patients on pump therapy, with basal insulin significantly reduced by day 32 (PMID: 41144928).
- The TTT1 Phase 3 trial was designed to assess semaglutide (uptitrated to 1.0 mg weekly) plus insulin vs. standard insulin therapy in overweight/obese adults with T1D and above-target HbA1c (7.5%–11.0%), with safety outcomes including hypoglycemia and ketosis (PMID: 42473259).
- In a qualitative analysis from a crossover RCT, 42% of T1D participants expressed interest in continuing semaglutide outside the study, citing lower insulin requirements, weight loss, and improved glycemic management, while the Diabetes Bowel Symptom Questionnaire showed increased gastrointestinal symptom frequency and severity with semaglutide vs. placebo (PMID: 41175929).

### Alcohol Abuse

#### Literature — Alcohol Abuse

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 9

Multiple randomized controlled trials and observational studies provide evidence that semaglutide reduces alcohol consumption, craving, and heavy drinking days in adults with alcohol use disorder (AUD). A phase 2 RCT of oral semaglutide (PMID: 42522065) significantly reduced heavy drinking days, drinks per drinking day, and naturalistic craving versus placebo. A phase 2 RCT of once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (PMID: 39937469) reduced laboratory alcohol self-administration, drinks per drinking day, and weekly craving. A 26-week RCT in patients with AUD and comorbid obesity (PMID: 42070571) showed semaglutide significantly reduced heavy drinking days versus placebo. A large real-world retrospective cohort study (PMID: 38806481) found semaglutide associated with 50–56% lower risk of AUD incidence and recurrence. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PMID: 41273789, 39969054, 41350683) note that while RCT evidence for semaglutide is promising, larger trials are needed to confirm effects, and observational data consistently show reduced alcohol-related events.

**Key findings:**
- Oral semaglutide (3–7 mg/day, 8 weeks) significantly reduced heavy drinking days (b=−0.580, 95% CI=−1.012 to −0.148), drinks per drinking day, naturalistic alcohol craving, and alcohol-related consequences versus placebo in adults with moderate-to-severe AUD (PMID: 42522065).
- Once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide reduced grams of alcohol consumed during a laboratory self-administration task (β=−0.48, 95% CI=−0.85 to −0.11, P=0.01), drinks per drinking day (β=−0.41, P=0.04), and weekly alcohol craving (β=−0.39, P=0.01) versus placebo in non-treatment-seeking adults with AUD (PMID: 39937469).
- Once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg (26 weeks) significantly reduced heavy drinking days by −41.1 percentage points from baseline versus −26.4 for placebo (estimated treatment difference −13.7 percentage points, p=0.0015) in treatment-seeking patients with AUD and comorbid obesity (PMID: 42070571).
- In a retrospective cohort of 83,825 patients with obesity, semaglutide was associated with 50–56% lower risk of both incidence and recurrence of AUD over 12 months compared with other anti-obesity medications, with consistent reductions across gender, age, race, and diabetes status (PMID: 38806481).
- A review of three RCTs found semaglutide reduced alcohol use in AUD populations, while noting that large RCTs are still needed to substantiate the role of GLP-1 RAs in treating AUD (PMID: 41273789).
- A systematic review of preclinical and clinical studies found semaglutide associated with reduced alcohol intake in human studies with AUD, though effects were sometimes transient, and called for large well-controlled studies (PMID: 39969054).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs and observational studies found semaglutide showed greater craving reduction than other GLP-1RAs (p=0.024), and observational studies showed reduced alcohol-related events (HR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.59–0.69), though RCT effects on consumption and craving were non-significant in pooled analysis (PMID: 41350683).
- A published trial protocol (SEMALCO trial) describes a 26-week randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial of once-weekly semaglutide in 108 patients with AUD and comorbid obesity, with primary endpoint of reduction in heavy drinking days, indicating active clinical investigation (PMID: 39779270).
- A narrative review concluded that semaglutide significantly reduces alcohol consumption and relapse of alcohol addiction in rats and has shown potential effectiveness for treating alcohol abuse in humans, particularly in overweight patients with AUD, while noting risks such as gallbladder disease and delayed gastric emptying (PMID: 40228539).

### Stroke

#### Literature — Stroke

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

Multiple lines of evidence support semaglutide for stroke prevention and treatment. In post-hoc analyses of two cardiovascular outcome RCTs (SUSTAIN 6 and PIONEER 6), semaglutide significantly reduced the incidence of any first stroke versus placebo in people with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk, primarily driven by small-vessel occlusion prevention (PMID: 35582947). A phase 2 RCT in patients with acute large vessel occlusion undergoing endovascular therapy found semaglutide was safe and associated with improved neurological outcomes in patients not receiving intravenous thrombolysis, though the primary endpoint was not met overall (PMID: 41392086). Preclinical rat stroke models demonstrated that semaglutide reduced infarct size, neurological impairment, inflammation, apoptosis, and normalized neurogenesis (PMID: 31465784), and a broader review of preclinical and clinical evidence confirmed neuroprotective mechanisms and stroke risk reduction (PMID: 42573665). A meta-analysis of semaglutide-specific RCTs in type 2 diabetes also reported a 32% reduction in nonfatal stroke risk (PMID: 41188987).

**Key findings:**
- In pooled post-hoc analysis of SUSTAIN 6 and PIONEER 6 RCTs (n=6480), semaglutide reduced incidence of any first stroke versus placebo (0.8 vs 1.1 events/100 PYO; HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.46–1.00; P=0.048), driven by significant reduction in small-vessel occlusion (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29–0.89; P=0.017) (PMID: 35582947).
- Semaglutide reduced stroke risk irrespective of prior stroke at baseline, with HR 0.60 (95% CI 0.37–0.99) in those without prior stroke and 0.89 (95% CI 0.47–1.69) in those with prior stroke (PMID: 35582947).
- In a phase 2 RCT of patients with acute large vessel occlusion undergoing endovascular therapy (n=140), semaglutide did not significantly improve the primary outcome overall (adjusted RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.95–1.15, p=0.37), but in the no-intravenous-thrombolysis stratum, favorable neurological recovery occurred in 64.7% vs 44.1% (adjusted RR 1.18, 95% CI 1.02–1.36), with no severe adverse events attributed to semaglutide (PMID: 41392086).
- In a rat middle cerebral artery occlusion model, semaglutide (10 nmol/kg ip starting 2h post-surgery) significantly reduced neurological impairment scores, cerebral infarction size, hippocampal neuron loss, activated microglia, p38 MAPK–NF-κB inflammation signaling, apoptosis markers, and normalized neurogenesis (DCX-positive cells) in the dentate gyrus (PMID: 31465784).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of semaglutide-specific RCTs in type 2 diabetes (17 studies, n=40,632) reported a 32% reduction in nonfatal stroke risk (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.56–0.83) (PMID: 41188987).
- A review of preclinical and clinical evidence noted that semaglutide has reported neuroprotective effects in stroke models, reducing neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress markers, and that observational studies suggest an association between GLP-1 RA exposure and lower dementia-related risk, though causality has not been established (PMID: 42573665).

### Nicotine Dependence

#### Literature — Nicotine Dependence

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 3

A phase 2a randomized clinical trial of subcutaneous semaglutide in non-treatment-seeking smokers found that semaglutide did not significantly improve the co-primary laboratory endpoints of smoking resistance or weekly cigarettes per day, but did significantly reduce nicotine craving and body weight, with supplementary analyses suggesting reduced laboratory smoking (PMID: 42189538). **Key findings:**
- In a phase 2a RCT of 24 randomized participants, semaglutide did not significantly improve co-primary laboratory outcomes of smoking resistance (β=0.16, P=0.16) or number of cigarettes self-administered (β=-0.08, P=0.30) compared to placebo (PMID: 42189538).

- Semaglutide significantly reduced cigarette craving over treatment weeks (treatment-by-time interaction: β=-0.11, P=0.01) and body weight (β=-0.04, P<0.001) in the phase 2a RCT (PMID: 42189538).
- A systematic review following PRISMA guidelines found that semaglutide showed indirect behavioral benefits in real-world observational data for smoking cessation and concluded that GLP-1 RAs including semaglutide exhibit promising neurobehavioral and metabolic effects warranting larger trials (PMID: 41697556).
- An ongoing double-blind RCT protocol (NCT06173778) is randomizing 177 treatment-seeking smokers with overweight/obesity to semaglutide 2.4 mg once-weekly vs placebo for 28 weeks, with primary outcome of percent body weight change and exploratory outcomes including smoking abstinence rates (PMID: 40554081).

**Key findings:**
- Semaglutide combined with metformin for 16 weeks produced significantly greater weight loss (6.09 ± 3.34 kg vs. 2.25 ± 4.27 kg), greater reductions in testosterone and CRP, higher menstrual cycle recovery rates, and a significantly higher natural pregnancy rate (35% vs. 15%) compared to metformin monotherapy in overweight/obese women with PCOS (PMID: 40713699).

### Myocardial Infarction

#### Literature — Myocardial Infarction

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 12

Multiple large RCTs demonstrate that semaglutide reduces the composite endpoint of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which includes nonfatal myocardial infarction, across diverse populations including patients with obesity without diabetes (PMID: 37952131), patients with type 2 diabetes at high CV risk (PMID: 27633186, 40162642), and patients with heart failure (PMID: 39181597, 41627802). The evidence supports the use of semaglutide in reducing myocardial infarction risk as a component of MACE, though individual MI endpoint results were not always statistically significant on their own (PMID: 27633186, 31185157). Overall, the body of evidence supports a cardioprotective effect of semaglutide that encompasses reduction of nonfatal myocardial infarction.

**Key findings:**
- In the SELECT trial (17,604 patients with obesity and established CVD without diabetes), semaglutide reduced MACE (including nonfatal MI) with HR 0.80 (95% CI 0.72–0.90, P<0.001) versus placebo (PMID: 37952131).
- In the SUSTAIN-6 trial, nonfatal MI occurred in 2.9% of semaglutide-treated patients vs 3.9% of placebo patients (HR 0.74; 95% CI 0.51–1.08; P=0.12), a nonsignificant trend favoring semaglutide (PMID: 27633186).
- In the PIONEER 6 trial, nonfatal MI occurred in 2.3% of oral semaglutide patients vs 1.9% of placebo patients (HR 1.18; 95% CI 0.73–1.90), with no significant difference (PMID: 31185157).
- In the SOUL trial, oral semaglutide significantly reduced MACE (including nonfatal MI) in people with T2D and ASCVD/CKD (HR 0.86; 95% CI 0.77–0.96; P=0.006) (PMID: 40162642).
- A prespecified SELECT analysis found semaglutide reduced MACE (HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.60–0.87) in patients with heart failure, with consistent benefits regardless of heart failure subtype (PMID: 39181597).
- A pooled post-hoc analysis of SUSTAIN 6 and PIONEER 6 found semaglutide significantly reduced primary MACE risk consistently across subgroups defined by prior CVD status and baseline BMI (PMID: 40704485).
- A prespecified SELECT analysis found semaglutide reduced MACE consistently across all baseline weight and waist circumference categories, with an estimated 33% of the MACE benefit mediated through waist circumference reduction (PMID: 41138739).
- In a prespecified SELECT analysis of patients at high risk for liver fibrosis, semaglutide reduced MACE by 26% (HR 0.74; 95% CI 0.63–0.88; P=0.0004) versus placebo (PMID: 41928037).
- An exploratory SELECT analysis found semaglutide reduced total hospital admissions (18.3 vs 20.4 per 100 patient-years; MR 0.90; 95% CI 0.85–0.95; P<0.001) and days hospitalized, extending benefits beyond CV risk reduction alone (PMID: 41433034).
- A prespecified SELECT analysis by baseline HbA1c showed semaglutide reduced cardiovascular events (including nonfatal MI as part of MACE) irrespective of baseline HbA1c or change in HbA1c in patients with overweight/obesity and established ASCVD without diabetes (PMID: 38907684).
- In the SOUL trial, oral semaglutide reduced MACE independently of concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor use (HR 0.84; 95% CI 0.74–0.95 in non-SGLT2i users; P-interaction 0.66) (PMID: 40156843).
- A secondary analysis of the SOUL trial found oral semaglutide reduced a composite HF outcome (HR 0.78; 95% CI 0.63–0.96) in participants with HF at baseline, with no heterogeneity in MACE risk reduction between those with and without HF history (PMID: 41627802).

### Liver Disorder

#### Literature — Liver Disorder

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The evidence supports the use of semaglutide for liver disorders, primarily through reductions in liver fat content and liver enzyme markers. In a randomized trial comparing semaglutide to dulaglutide in type 2 diabetes patients, semaglutide significantly reduced alanine aminotransferase, γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, and liver fat percentage as measured by the liver-to-spleen ratio on CT (PMID: 37646192). In a phase IIa active-comparator trial in NAFLD patients, semaglutide 1 mg weekly produced a 42.3% relative reduction in liver fat content, though efinopegdutide was superior (PMID: 37355043). Pharmacokinetic studies confirm that hepatic impairment does not alter semaglutide exposure for either subcutaneous (PMID: 29205786) or oral formulations (PMID: 29693715), suggesting no dose adjustment is needed in patients with liver disease. A Cochrane review of semaglutide for obesity also listed liver disease-related outcomes as an important outcome domain (PMID: 41161683).

**Key findings:**
- In a 24-week randomized trial in Japanese type 2 diabetes patients, semaglutide significantly reduced alanine aminotransferase and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase compared to dulaglutide, and only the semaglutide group showed a significant elevation in the liver-to-spleen ratio (indicating reduced liver fat) on CT (PMID: 37646192).
- In a phase IIa randomized active-comparator trial in NAFLD patients, semaglutide 1 mg weekly produced a least-squares mean relative reduction from baseline in liver fat content of 42.3% at 24 weeks, though this was significantly less than efinopegdutide (72.7%) (PMID: 37355043).
- A pharmacokinetic study of subcutaneous semaglutide 0.5 mg in participants with mild, moderate, or severe hepatic impairment showed AUC0-∞ ratios of 0.95, 1.02, and 0.97 respectively versus normal hepatic function, indicating no clinically relevant effect of hepatic impairment on semaglutide exposure (PMID: 29205786).
- A pharmacokinetic study of oral semaglutide in subjects with mild, moderate, or severe hepatic impairment found that AUC and Cmax appeared similar across all hepatic function groups, with no safety concerns identified, suggesting dose adjustment is not warranted (PMID: 29693715).
- A Cochrane systematic review of 18 RCTs of semaglutide for obesity included liver disease-related outcomes as an important outcome domain, reflecting recognized hepatic relevance of semaglutide treatment (PMID: 41161683).

### Alzheimer Disease

#### Literature — Alzheimer Disease

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

The evidence for semaglutide in Alzheimer's disease is mixed overall. The largest and most definitive evidence comes from two phase 3 RCTs (evoke and evoke+) in which oral semaglutide 14 mg failed to slow clinical progression of early Alzheimer's disease on the primary endpoint (CDR-SB at week 104), leading to trial discontinuation (PMID: 41865758). A reanalysis of those same trials suggested limited drug effects at later time points (weeks 130 and 156) on some secondary measures, and a systematic review/meta-analysis reported neuroprotective signals and reduced AD risk compared to various treatments (PMID: 42261705; PMID: 41766347). Preclinical data in 3xTg mice showed semaglutide improved cognition and glucose metabolism via GLP-1R/SIRT1/GLUT4 signaling (PMID: 37730113), but a separate study in 5xFAD mice found no effect on memory, amyloid plaque deposition, or glial activation (PMID: 42413499).

**Key findings:**
- In two phase 3 RCTs (evoke and evoke+, n=3808 total), oral semaglutide 14 mg did not significantly change CDR-SB scores from baseline to week 104 compared to placebo (estimated differences: -0.08 [95% CI -0.35 to 0.20], p=0.57 in evoke; 0.10 [-0.17 to 0.38], p=0.46 in evoke+), and both trials were discontinued due to negative clinical outcomes (PMID: 41865758).
- A reanalysis of the evoke and evoke+ trial data found that the ADCS-ADL-MCI test in the evoke trial at week 130 showed a statistically significant difference favoring semaglutide (p=0.0039), and some CSF biomarker analyses showed significant differences in AD markers, suggesting limited drug effects at later time points (PMID: 42261705).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis found that semaglutide demonstrated neuroprotective effects, lowering the risk of AD compared to various treatments, though the authors noted high heterogeneity across included studies (PMID: 41766347).
- In 3xTg mice, semaglutide treatment (alternate-day injections for 30 days) improved learning and memory, decreased amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, and increased SIRT1 and GLUT4 expression in the hippocampus, with effects blocked by a SIRT1 inhibitor (PMID: 37730113).
- In 5xFAD mice treated with semaglutide for 2 or 4 months, neither memory/learning tasks, amyloid-β plaque deposition, nor glial cell activation showed measurable improvement despite reductions in body weight and improved glucose tolerance; semaglutide also did not alter microglial activation or inflammatory markers in an LPS model (PMID: 42413499).

### Coronary Artery Disorder

#### Literature — Coronary Artery Disorder

**Evidence strength:** moderate, supports
**Relevant studies:** 2

The evidence supports repurposing semaglutide for coronary artery disorder. In a prespecified analysis of the SELECT trial, semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (including cardiovascular mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and stroke) by 20% versus placebo in people with overweight/obesity and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with consistent benefit irrespective of baseline HbA1c or HbA1c change (PMID: 38907684). **Key findings:**
- In the SELECT trial prespecified analysis, semaglutide reduced the primary endpoint of first MACE (cardiovascular mortality, nonfatal MI, or stroke) by 20% versus placebo in people with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and overweight/obesity without diabetes (PMID: 38907684).

- Semaglutide also reduced expanded MACE (including coronary revascularization and hospitalization for unstable angina) and a heart failure composite, with consistent results across HbA1c subgroups (PMID: 38907684).
- Applying SELECT trial risk reduction estimates to the MESA cohort, the 5-year number-needed-to-treat for MACE with semaglutide was 79 for individuals with CAC ≥300 versus 653 for those with CAC=0, among people without diabetes or established CVD (PMID: 39797878).

### Parkinson Disease

#### Literature — Parkinson Disease

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 4

Evidence for semaglutide specifically in Parkinson's disease is limited but emerging. A registered phase 2 RCT (MOST-ABLE study) is underway testing oral semaglutide (7 mg and 14 mg) in idiopathic PD patients, with the primary endpoint being MDS-UPDRS Part III off-medication score change at 48 weeks (PMID: 41448692). Two systematic reviews and meta-analyses that included semaglutide among GLP-1 RAs in PD found no statistically significant overall benefit on motor outcomes (MDS-UPDRS Part III off- or on-medication), though one noted a borderline significant improvement in on-medication motor scores across GLP-1 RAs as a class (PMID: 41880197, PMID: 40835782, PMID: 41631108). The evidence for semaglutide specifically treating Parkinson's disease is therefore mixed and preliminary, with no completed semaglutide-specific PD trial results yet available.

**Key findings:**
- The MOST-ABLE study is a phase 2, multicentre, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial of oral semaglutide tablets (7 mg or 14 mg once daily) in 99 participants with idiopathic PD (Hoehn & Yahr stages 1–2.5), with the primary endpoint being change in MDS-UPDRS Part III off-medication score from baseline at 48 weeks (PMID: 41448692).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of five RCTs (708 participants) evaluating GLP-1 RAs in PD found no statistically significant differences in MDS-UPDRS Part III scores off medication (MD: -2.00, 95% CI: -4.12 to 0.11, p=0.06) or on medication (MD: -1.40, 95% CI: -3.42 to 0.62, p=0.17), with GLP-1 RAs associated with increased gastrointestinal side effects including nausea (RR: 2.09) and vomiting (RR: 4.53) (PMID: 41880197).
- A meta-analysis of four placebo-controlled RCTs found GLP-1 RAs demonstrated a statistically significant overall improvement in on-medication motor scores (MDS-UPDRS Part III: MD -0.75, 95% CI [-1.50, -0.0009]; P=0.04), but no significant overall improvement in off-medication motor scores (MD -1.39; P=0.16), with no significant differences in PDQ-39 or levodopa equivalent daily dose (PMID: 40835782).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of four RCTs (667 PD patients) found GLP-1 RAs were not associated with improvements in MDS-UPDRS Part III in either off-medication (SMD: -0.16; 95% CI: -0.64 to 0.32; p=0.52) or on-medication states (SMD: -0.13; 95% CI: -0.51 to 0.25; p=0.49), nor in other MDS-UPDRS subscores, Non-Motor Symptoms Scale, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, or PDQ-39 scores, though GLP-1 RAs were associated with increased risk of weight loss (RR: 1.44; 95% CI: 1.04–1.99; p=0.03) (PMID: 41631108).

### Schizophrenia

#### Literature — Schizophrenia

**Evidence strength:** strong, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 5

Multiple randomized, placebo-controlled trials of semaglutide in patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders demonstrate consistent and significant improvements in cardiometabolic outcomes (HbA1c, body weight, insulin sensitivity) but no significant effect on core psychiatric symptoms as measured by PANSS-6 (PMID: 40900607, PMID: 41335431, PMID: 40506208, PMID: 41778920). Physical quality of life improved significantly with semaglutide in schizophrenia patients, though mental quality of life and psychiatric symptom severity were not meaningfully changed (PMID: 41702353, PMID: 40900607). The evidence supports semaglutide as a cardiometabolic intervention in schizophrenia but does not support it as a treatment for the core psychotic symptoms of schizophrenia itself.

**Key findings:**
- In the HISTORI trial (n=154), semaglutide reduced HbA1c by 0.46% and body weight by 9.21 kg versus placebo in SGA-treated patients with schizophrenia and prediabetes, but had no significant effect on PANSS-6 score or mental quality of life (PMID: 40900607).
- Semaglutide improved physical quality of life (SF-36v2 PCS) at weeks 15 and 30 with effect sizes exceeding the minimally important difference in schizophrenia patients, but no total or mediated effects were found for mental component summary scores or PANSS-6 (PMID: 41702353).
- In a multicenter RCT of individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders on clozapine or olanzapine, semaglutide significantly reduced HbA1c (mean difference -0.25%) and body weight (-9.2 kg) versus placebo, with no differences in psychiatric symptoms between groups (PMID: 41335431).
- Semaglutide significantly improved insulin sensitivity, reduced insulin resistance, and lowered fasting glucose in patients with schizophrenia and prediabetes on second-generation antipsychotics; weight loss partly mediated metabolic improvements, while β-cell function remained largely unchanged (PMID: 41778920).
- In the COaST trial (n=31), semaglutide yielded a 13.88% body weight reduction versus 0.42% for placebo in clozapine-treated patients with schizophrenia and obesity, with no differences in PANSS scores or clozapine/norclozapine concentrations, and no serious treatment-related adverse events (PMID: 40506208).

### Hypoglycemia

#### Literature — Hypoglycemia

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 3

The available evidence does not support repurposing semaglutide to treat hypoglycemia; rather, the evidence indicates semaglutide does not impair and may modestly reduce hypoglycemia risk when used in diabetes management contexts. A randomized crossover trial found that semaglutide did not compromise the counterregulatory glucagon response during experimental hypoglycemia in type 2 diabetes (PMID: 29893488), and a phase 3b trial showed semaglutide added to dose-reduced insulin glargine significantly reduced severe hypoglycemia rates compared to dose-titrated insulin glargine alone (PMID: 42504064). In adults with type 1 diabetes and obesity, semaglutide resulted in only two severe hypoglycemia events per group, comparable to placebo (PMID: 40550013), providing no evidence that semaglutide treats hypoglycemia as a primary indication.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial, semaglutide did not compromise the counterregulatory glucagon response during experimental hypoglycemia in people with type 2 diabetes; the absolute change in mean glucagon concentration from 5.5 mmol/L to nadir was similar for semaglutide vs placebo (88.3 vs 83.1 pg/mL, estimated difference 5.2 pg/mL, 95% CI -7.7 to 18.1) (PMID: 29893488).
- Noradrenaline and cortisol counterregulatory hormone increases were statistically significantly lower with semaglutide versus placebo during hypoglycemic clamp, and mean hypoglycemic symptom scores were lower with semaglutide, though cognitive function test results were similar (PMID: 29893488).
- In a phase 3b RCT, semaglutide 2.0 mg added to dose-reduced insulin glargine reduced severe hypoglycemia rates compared to dose-titrated insulin glargine alone (rate ratio: 0.45; 95% CI: 0.23, 0.87; p=0.02), indicating semaglutide reduces hypoglycemia risk rather than treating it (PMID: 42504064).
- In a 26-week double-blind RCT of adults with type 1 diabetes and obesity, there were two severe hypoglycemia events in each of the semaglutide and placebo groups, and no diabetic ketoacidosis was reported, indicating semaglutide did not increase or treat hypoglycemia (PMID: 40550013).

### Eye Disorder

#### Literature — Eye Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 12

The evidence for semaglutide in eye disorders is mixed, with signals of both potential benefit and harm across multiple ocular conditions. A large meta-analysis of 78 RCTs (73,640 participants) found semaglutide did not increase overall eye disorder or diabetic retinopathy risk, but was associated with a significant odds of nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) (OR 3.92; 95% CI 1.02–15.02), though evidence was insufficient for definitive conclusions (PMID: 40810985). Conversely, preclinical data show semaglutide protects retinal ganglion cells against metabolic degeneration (PMID: 41665299), alleviates age-related dry eye disease in mouse models (PMID: 41812831), and epidemiological reviews suggest GLP-1 receptor agonists including semaglutide may reduce glaucoma incidence (PMID: 39732312), while the drug's association with diabetic retinopathy worsening and NAION remains a clinical concern requiring further study (PMID: 39883468, PMID: 40289093).

**Key findings:**
- A meta-analysis of 78 RCTs (73,640 participants) found semaglutide did not increase the risk of eye disorders (OR 1.01; 95% CI 0.91–1.12) or diabetic retinopathy (OR 1.04; 95% CI 0.92–1.17), but was associated with a significant odds of NAION (OR 3.92; 95% CI 1.02–15.02); TSA indicated insufficient sample size for definitive NAION conclusions (PMID: 40810985).
- A randomized clinical trial found semaglutide increased central retinal thickness by ~1% (3.8 μm; 95% CI 0.9–6.7; p=0.009) compared to placebo, with uncertain clinical significance (PMID: 37062189).
- A post-hoc analysis of a randomized trial found semaglutide increased central retinal thickness by ~1% (3.76 μm; p=0.065 vs placebo), but this was attenuated after adjustment for time in range, suggesting the effect may be mediated by glycemic improvement rather than a direct drug effect (PMID: 40239470).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 observational studies (n=4,831,654) found no statistically significant overall increase in NAION risk with semaglutide vs non-GLP-1 RA comparators (OR 2.44; 95% CI 0.59–10.15; very low certainty), though subgroup analysis in type 2 diabetes showed a pooled aHR of 1.70 (95% CI 1.10–2.64; low certainty) (PMID: 41692115).
- A retrospective case series reported 7 cases of nonarteritic ischemic anterior optic neuropathy, 1 case of bilateral papillitis, and 1 case of paracentral acute middle maculopathy associated with semaglutide or tirzepatide use; causality could not be established (PMID: 39883468).
- FAERS database analysis identified 1,733 ocular adverse event reports associated with semaglutide; subcutaneous injections showed higher frequency of retinal complications (diabetic retinopathy, ischemic optic neuropathy, retinal detachment, retinal tear, retinal hemorrhage) compared to oral formulations, with most cases occurring within the first month (PMID: 40289093).
- A meta-analysis of 7 studies (242,537 patients) found GLP-1RA use was associated with significantly decreased risk of diabetic retinopathy incidence compared to insulin (RR=0.66; 95% CI 0.48–0.91; p=0.01), but increased risk of DR complications compared to oral antidiabetic medications (RR=1.39; 95% CI 1.07–1.80; p=0.01) (PMID: 39327045).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 5 retrospective studies (156,042 participants) found GLP-1 RA use was associated with reduced glaucoma incidence when one influential study was excluded (HR 0.71; 95% CI 0.60–0.85; I²=29%), though overall results were not statistically significant with all studies included (PMID: 39732312).
- A comprehensive review found GLP-1RAs including semaglutide show a largely consistent association with reduced glaucoma incidence, potential long-term neurovascular protection in diabetic retinopathy (with risk of transient early worsening), and dichotomous findings in age-related macular degeneration (protective against non-exudative forms, potential increased risk for neovascular disease) (PMID: 41501669).
- In a mouse model, semaglutide pretreatment prevented rotenone-induced retinal ganglion cell loss and enhanced glucose metabolism, suggesting potential neuroprotective effects relevant to glaucoma (PMID: 41665299).
- In a naturally aged mouse model, long-term semaglutide treatment significantly alleviated dry eye disease phenotypes, preserved lacrimal gland structural integrity, reduced senescent cell burden, and decreased systemic inflammatory cytokines (PMID: 41812831).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 retrospective cohort studies found GLP-1RA use was not associated with increased diabetic macular edema risk compared to mixed antihyperglycemic therapies (pooled HR 0.81; 95% CI 0.52–1.26), though GLP-1RAs showed higher relative risk compared to SGLT-2 inhibitors (HR 1.50; 95% CI 1.17–1.94); evidence certainty was very low (PMID: 42242293).
- A literature review noted that in a pivotal trial, semaglutide was associated with approximately 75% increased risk of diabetic retinopathy worsening, and dedicated safety studies for semaglutide in diabetic retinopathy are currently underway; cases of worsening were rare and most vulnerable patients are those with preexisting high-risk DR and poor baseline glycemic control (PMID: 39151204).
