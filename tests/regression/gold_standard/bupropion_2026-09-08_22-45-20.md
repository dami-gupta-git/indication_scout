## Findings by Disease

### Binge Eating Disorder

#### Literature — Binge Eating Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 5

Multiple randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of bupropion (alone or in combination with naltrexone) for binge-eating disorder consistently show that bupropion does not significantly reduce binge-eating frequency or achieve binge-eating remission relative to placebo (PMID: 23656848, PMID: 37751990, PMID: 33218742). One trial found that naltrexone/bupropion was significantly superior to placebo for binge-eating remission when combined with behavioral weight loss therapy, and that naltrexone/bupropion maintenance after acute naltrexone/bupropion response preserved remission, but bupropion alone or the combination without behavioral therapy did not consistently outperform placebo on binge-eating outcomes (PMID: 36285406, PMID: 37366017). The evidence contradicts the use of bupropion as a stand-alone treatment for binge-eating disorder, though it consistently produces modest weight loss in this population.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled 8-week trial of bupropion 300 mg/day vs. placebo in 61 overweight/obese women with BED, there were no significant differences between bupropion and placebo on binge-eating frequency, food craving, or eating disorder psychopathology; only weight loss favored bupropion (1.8% vs. 0.6% BMI loss, P=.002) (PMID: 23656848).
- Authors of PMID 23656848 concluded that findings do not support bupropion as a stand-alone treatment for BED (PMID: 23656848).
- In a 12-week randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial of naltrexone/bupropion vs. placebo in 89 patients with BED, mixed models of binge-eating frequency and logistic regression of binge-eating remission rates did not differ significantly between naltrexone/bupropion and placebo; naltrexone/bupropion produced significantly greater weight loss (PMID: 37751990).
- In a pilot RCT of naltrexone/bupropion (NB 50/300 mg) vs. placebo for 12 weeks in 22 patients with BED and obesity, significant reductions in binge eating occurred in both groups but did not differ significantly between NB and placebo; the percentage attaining ≥3% weight loss was significantly greater with NB (45.5% vs. 0%) (PMID: 33218742).
- In a 2×2 factorial randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial (N=136) testing naltrexone-bupropion and behavioral weight loss therapy (BWL) alone and combined for BED with obesity, intention-to-treat binge-eating remission rates were 17.7% (placebo), 31.3% (naltrexone-bupropion alone), 37.1% (BWL+placebo), and 57.1% (BWL+naltrexone-bupropion); naltrexone-bupropion was significantly superior to placebo for binge-eating remission, but BWL was also independently superior and the interaction was not significant (PMID: 36285406).
- In a 16-week maintenance trial of naltrexone/bupropion vs. placebo in 66 responders to acute treatments for BED with obesity, intention-to-treat binge-eating remission rates were 68.8% for naltrexone/bupropion vs. 50.0% for placebo; placebo following acute naltrexone/bupropion response was associated with significantly decreased probability of binge-eating remission and increased binge-eating frequency (PMID: 37366017).
- Naltrexone/bupropion following response to acute naltrexone/bupropion treatment was associated with good maintenance of binge-eating remission, low binge-eating frequency, and significant additional weight loss (PMID: 37366017).

### Attention Deficit-hyperactivity Disorder

#### Literature — Attention Deficit-hyperactivity Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 13

Multiple controlled clinical trials demonstrate that bupropion is effective for treating ADHD across age groups and formulations. In adults, bupropion SR and bupropion XL both significantly outperformed placebo on ADHD symptom measures (PMID: 11156812, PMID: 15820237, PMID: 25325205), and in children, bupropion showed significant treatment effects versus placebo and comparable efficacy to methylphenidate (PMID: 8885585, PMID: 7775360, PMID: 22806822). One trial in methadone-maintained patients with comorbid ADHD found no significant advantage of bupropion SR over placebo, introducing some mixed evidence (PMID: 16102908), but the overall body of evidence supports bupropion as an effective nonstimulant treatment for ADHD. Minor but statistically significant increases in blood pressure and heart rate were noted with bupropion treatment (PMID: 15705013).

**Key findings:**
- In a double-blind, placebo-controlled 6-week RCT in adults with DSM-IV ADHD, bupropion SR produced a 42% reduction in ADHD symptoms vs. 24% for placebo, with 76% of bupropion-treated subjects responding vs. 37% on placebo (PMID: 11156812).
- In a multisite, double-blind, placebo-controlled 8-week RCT, bupropion XL responders (53%) significantly exceeded placebo responders (31%; p=.004), with a treatment effect size of 0.6 on the ADHD Rating Scale (PMID: 15820237).
- In a 6-week double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in adults, bupropion 150 mg/day produced significantly greater reductions in CAARS scores than placebo after 6 weeks (PMID: 25325205).
- In a multisite, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in children aged 6-12 with ADDH, bupropion showed significant treatment effects on conduct problems and hyperactivity as early as day 3 on teacher ratings, and on parent ratings at day 28 (PMID: 8885585).
- In a double-blind crossover trial in youth aged 7-17, bupropion and methylphenidate produced equivalent and significantly greater improvement (p<.001) on the Iowa-Conners Teacher's Rating Scale and Clinical Global Impression Scale (PMID: 7775360).
- In a 6-week randomized double-blind study in children and adolescents, bupropion showed no significant difference from methylphenidate on Parent and Teacher ADHD-RS-IV scores, with 90% of each group achieving response on the Parent scale (PMID: 22806822).
- In a randomized double-blind parallel trial comparing bupropion SR, methylphenidate, and placebo in adults with ADHD, response rates were 64% for bupropion, 50% for methylphenidate, and 27% for placebo, though the difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.14) (PMID: 11791949).
- In a 12-week double-blind, three-arm trial in methadone-maintained patients with adult ADHD, sustained-release bupropion did not provide a significant advantage over placebo in reducing ADHD symptoms, with a high placebo response rate of 46% (PMID: 16102908).
- An open trial of bupropion in 19 adults with ADHD found that 14 of 19 patients experienced moderate to marked benefit, and 10 chose to continue bupropion over their former medication (PMID: 2115746).
- A 12-week single-blind trial of bupropion in 11 patients with cocaine dependence and adult ADHD found significant reductions in attention difficulties, hyperactivity, and impulsivity (PMID: 11916368).
- A 5-week open-label bupropion trial in 13 adolescent boys with ADHD, conduct disorder, and substance use disorders showed significant declines in Conners Hyperactivity Index (75.5 to 65.4, p<.01) and Clinical Global Impressions Severity scores (4.9 to 3.0, p<.002) (PMID: 9847499).
- In healthy male volunteers, bupropion at 150 mg/day increased composedness, agreeability, and energy on the POMS scale, while 300 mg/day improved attention, with a behavioral profile distinct from methylphenidate (PMID: 12826985).
- Reanalysis of placebo-controlled ADHD studies found that bupropion treatment was associated with statistically significant increases in systolic blood pressure (+5.9 mm Hg, p<.05) and heart rate (+6.9 mm Hg, p<.05) compared to baseline (PMID: 15705013).

### Cocaine Use Disorder

#### Literature — Cocaine Use Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 9

The evidence for bupropion in cocaine use disorder is mixed across multiple study types. Clinical RCTs show that bupropion alone or combined with naltrexone did not significantly reduce cocaine self-administration in a human laboratory study (PMID: 36805862), and a 26-week RCT found no overall benefit of bupropion SR vs. placebo on cocaine abstinence, though a subgroup analysis suggested benefit in abstinence-initiation patients (PMID: 36920397). In contrast, large observational/retrospective analyses found bupropion associated with higher CUD remission rates (PMID: 37703770), a systematic review and meta-analysis found increased abstinence with bupropion across two RCTs (PMID: 31183685), and preclinical rat studies showed high-dose bupropion attenuated cocaine self-administration (PMID: 39881654), while nonhuman primate data were mixed depending on cocaine dose in the mixture (PMID: 31789555).

**Key findings:**
- In a human laboratory RCT (n=31), bupropion (0, 100, 200, 400 mg/day) alone or combined with naltrexone did not significantly decrease cocaine self-administration on either a progressive ratio or relapse procedure; low-dose bupropion (100 mg) blunted subjective 'Like Drug' and 'Stimulated' ratings (PMID: 36805862).
- In a 26-week double-blind RCT (n=80) of bupropion SR vs. placebo combined with financial abstinence incentives, no overall significant difference in cocaine abstinence was observed between medication groups; however, in the abstinence-initiation subgroup, bupropion SR outperformed placebo (66.7% vs. 30.0% abstinent at end of study, P=.04) (PMID: 36920397).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of pharmacotherapy for cocaine use disorder found increased abstinence with bupropion across 2 RCTs (RR 1.63, 95% CI 1.02–2.59), though the strength of evidence was rated low (PMID: 31183685).
- A nationwide retrospective cohort study (n=161,544 CUD+depression patients) found bupropion associated with higher CUD remission rates compared to other antidepressants (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.27–1.94), and also higher remission in CUD patients with co-occurring nicotine dependence (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.11–1.71) (PMID: 37703770).
- In male and female Wistar rats, systemic bupropion at 60 mg/kg attenuated cocaine self-administration during extended-access (6 h) sessions without altering locomotor activity or inactive lever presses; the highest dose was more efficacious in females during the first hour (PMID: 39881654).
- In rhesus monkeys, bupropion treatment dose-dependently decreased self-administration of nicotine combined with a low dose of cocaine (0.0032 mg/kg/inj), but when the cocaine dose in the mixture was higher (0.01 mg/kg/inj), bupropion attenuated self-administration in only a subset of subjects; the effective dosage also increased responding for cocaine alone and nicotine alone (PMID: 31789555).
- A narrative review concluded that bupropion shows promise among medications for cocaine use disorder, alongside psychostimulants, modafinil, topiramate, and disulfiram, but evidence is limited by heterogeneity, small sample sizes, and inconsistent results (PMID: 31008728).
- A review of bupropion and its analogs noted clinical studies supporting bupropion's benefit in cocaine dependence and described development of bupropion analogs as potential pharmacotherapies for cocaine addiction (PMID: 24484978).
- A health claims retrospective analysis found bupropion prescription increased odds of stimulant use disorder remission by 2.1 times (99% CI: 1.09–3.89) in amphetamine use disorder but not in cocaine use disorder specifically (PMID: 30815171).

### Bipolar Disorder

#### Literature — Bipolar Disorder

**Evidence strength:** strong, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 14

Multiple clinical trials and studies, including double-blind randomized controlled trials, have examined bupropion for bipolar depression (the depressive phase of bipolar disorder). The evidence is mixed: bupropion shows antidepressant efficacy comparable to other agents in acute bipolar depression (PMID: 7929019, PMID: 10701483, PMID: 12180276), and is associated with a lower risk of mood switching to mania/hypomania compared to venlafaxine (PMID: 16880481, PMID: 16449476), but a large placebo-controlled RCT found adjunctive antidepressant therapy (including bupropion) was not superior to mood stabilizer plus placebo for durable recovery (PMID: 17392295), and several studies document meaningful rates of manic/hypomanic switching with bupropion use (PMID: 1487473, PMID: 11912569, PMID: 16449476). PMID: 12842303 was excluded as it studied bupropion for ADHD (not bipolar disorder) in patients who happened to have bipolar disorder.

**Key findings:**
- In a double-blind trial, bupropion and desipramine showed similar antidepressant efficacy in bipolar depression, but mania/hypomania occurred in only 1/9 bupropion-treated patients vs 5/10 desipramine-treated patients (PMID: 7929019).
- In a 10-week randomized trial of 174 adults with bipolar depression, bupropion, sertraline, and venlafaxine showed similar acute response rates (49-53%) and remission rates (34-41%), but venlafaxine was associated with significantly higher risk of switching to hypomania/mania compared to bupropion or sertraline (PMID: 16880481).
- In a large double-blind placebo-controlled RCT (N=366), adjunctive antidepressant therapy (including bupropion) plus mood stabilizer did not produce significantly higher rates of durable recovery than mood stabilizer plus placebo (23.5% vs 27.3%, P=0.40), and rates of treatment-emergent affective switch were similar between groups (PMID: 17392295).
- In 228 acute randomized trials of bupropion, sertraline, or venlafaxine as adjuncts to mood stabilizers, threshold switches into hypomania or mania occurred in 11.4% and 7.9% of acute trials respectively; venlafaxine had the highest relative risk of switching and bupropion the lowest (PMID: 16449476).
- In a prospective double-blind study of 95 acute antidepressant treatment phases (bupropion, sertraline, or venlafaxine), 37% showed much or very much improved depression, but 14% of acute phases and 33% of continuation phases were associated with mood switches into hypomania or mania (PMID: 11912569).
- In a single-blind study, both topiramate and bupropion SR showed significant antidepressant response (56% and 59% respectively meeting ≥50% HDRS reduction) when added to mood stabilizers in bipolar I/II depression, with no cases of affective switch in either arm (PMID: 12180276).
- An open trial of bupropion in 11 bipolar depressed patients found 7/11 had moderate-to-marked improvement at 6 weeks, but 6/11 experienced manic or hypomanic episodes necessitating discontinuation, suggesting bupropion may pose similar mania-induction risks as other antidepressants (PMID: 1487473).
- An open trial of bupropion in 11 patients with bipolar or schizoaffective disorder found most patients maintained on bupropion alone or with low-dose neuroleptics/anxiolytics for 1 year or more showed little or no relapse and few side effects (PMID: 2856918).
- A double-blind study comparing idazoxan and bupropion in bipolar I depressed patients found both drugs produced significant improvement over time with approximately 50% reductions in Hamilton Depression scores (PMID: 10701483).
- Carbamazepine markedly decreased bupropion peak concentrations by 87% and 24-hour AUC by 90%, while valproate did not significantly affect bupropion concentrations, indicating important pharmacokinetic interactions relevant to combination therapy in bipolar disorder (PMID: 8830063).
- In a double-blind RCT of adjunctive antidepressant maintenance in bipolar I disorder, 31% of patients continuing antidepressants (escitalopram or bupropion XL) for 52 weeks had a mood episode vs 46% in the 8-week group (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.43-1.10, P=0.12), a non-significant difference; however, recurrence of depression was significantly lower in the 52-week group (17% vs 40%), while mania/hypomania was numerically higher (12% vs 6%) (PMID: 37530824).
- In a blinded continuation study, 69% of acute positive antidepressant responders (treated with mood stabilizer plus one of three antidepressants including bupropion) maintained positive response over 1 year, and the switch rate into mania (13%) was not higher than reported rates for mood stabilizer monotherapy (PMID: 19358785).
- A post-hoc reanalysis of STEP-BD data found that higher doses of adjunctive antidepressants (including bupropion) were associated with significantly greater durable recovery than low doses, without a significant increase in treatment-emergent affective switch (PMID: 26228414).
- An open-label add-on study of bupropion 150 mg/day in 5 depressed bipolar I patients with comorbid cocaine dependence showed improvement in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale scores after 4 weeks without significant increase in Young Mania Rating Scale scores compared to controls (PMID: 24434527).

### Post-traumatic Stress Disorder

#### Literature — Post-traumatic Stress Disorder

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 3

The evidence for bupropion in post-traumatic stress disorder is mixed. A placebo-controlled RCT found no significant between-group difference for bupropion SR versus placebo on PTSD symptoms, though a post hoc analysis suggested younger patients not on a concurrent antidepressant may benefit (PMID: 17414245). A systematic review and meta-analysis found that bupropion failed to differentiate from placebo for PTSD (PMID: 27126398). A small case series with a non-bupropion control group reported greater reductions in PTSD symptom severity and lower relapse rates in patients with comorbid PTSD and methamphetamine use disorder who received bupropion-containing regimens (PMID: 39175424).

**Key findings:**
- In an 8-week placebo-controlled RCT of bupropion SR in 30 patients with PTSD, no significant between-group differences were detected on PTSD symptom measures; a post hoc analysis suggested younger patients not on a concurrent antidepressant were more likely to respond to bupropion (PMID: 17414245).
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of pharmacotherapy for PTSD found that bupropion failed to differentiate from placebo in PTSD treatment (PMID: 27126398).
- A case series of four patients with comorbid PTSD and methamphetamine use disorder treated with bupropion-containing regimens showed PTSD Checklist-Civilian scores declining from 77 at initiation to 29 post-discharge, compared to minimal change (51 to 53) in a non-bupropion control group of 41 patients; relapse rates were also lower in the bupropion group (25.0% vs 48.8%) (PMID: 39175424).

### Neuropathic Pain

#### Literature — Neuropathic Pain

**Evidence strength:** moderate, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 4

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial found that bupropion SR (150–300 mg/day) significantly reduced neuropathic pain scores compared to placebo in 41 non-depressed patients, with 73% reporting improvement (PMID: 11706096). A narrative review also noted that bupropion shows promise in neuropathic pain treatment based on its noradrenaline and dopamine reuptake inhibition (PMID: 20185402). However, a separate randomized placebo-controlled crossover trial found no significant benefit of bupropion SR over placebo in patients with non-neuropathic chronic low back pain, and a rat model study demonstrated antihyperalgesic effects via spinal noradrenaline and dopamine mechanisms (PMID: 16202958; PMID: 25427287), suggesting efficacy may be specific to neuropathic rather than non-neuropathic pain.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of 41 non-depressed patients, bupropion SR reduced mean average pain scores by 1.7 points (from 5.7 to 4.0, p<0.001) compared to no change with placebo, with 73% of patients reporting improved or much improved neuropathic pain relief (PMID: 11706096).
- Pain relief with bupropion SR was statistically significant at week 2 (p<0.05) and continued through weeks 3–6 (p<0.001), with a significant decrease in pain interference with quality of life (PMID: 11706096).
- A narrative review identified bupropion as a specific inhibitor of neuronal noradrenaline reuptake and a weak inhibitor of dopamine reuptake, noting it shows promise in the treatment of neuropathic pain (PMID: 20185402).
- In a randomized, placebo-controlled crossover trial of 44 patients with chronic low back pain (only 3 of whom met criteria for neuropathic low back pain), bupropion SR was not significantly better than placebo on daily pain intensity ratings, weekly pain intensity ratings, McGill Pain Questionnaire Present Pain Intensity, or pain relief ratings (PMID: 16202958).
- Intrathecal bupropion produced a dose-dependent antihyperalgesic effect in a rat spinal nerve ligation model of neuropathic pain (p<0.001), mediated through spinal α2-adrenoceptors and dopamine D2 receptors, with microdialysis confirming increased noradrenaline and dopamine concentrations in the spinal dorsal horn (PMID: 25427287).

### Narcolepsy

#### Literature — Narcolepsy

**Evidence strength:** weak, supports
**Relevant studies:** 2

Two case reports describe bupropion being used to treat narcolepsy (with comorbid depression), both reporting positive outcomes. In one case, bupropion 100 mg three times daily normalized mood, objective sleepiness (mean sleep latency improved from 1.8 to 9.1 minutes), and REM-sleep propensity in a narcoleptic woman with atypical depression, with reversion upon discontinuation (PMID: 9614600). In a second case, bupropion 300 mg/day rendered a narcoleptic patient with cataplexy and depression asymptomatic after 3 months, following failure of imipramine and modafinil (PMID: 24930928). The evidence supports repurposing bupropion for narcolepsy but is limited to case reports.

**Key findings:**
- Bupropion 100 mg three times daily normalized sleepiness (mean sleep latency improved from 1.8 to 9.1 minutes), REM-sleep propensity, and mood (Beck Depression Inventory from 24 to 6) in a 46-year-old narcoleptic woman with atypical depression; all parameters reverted to pretreatment levels upon discontinuation (PMID: 9614600).
- The authors of PMID 9614600 proposed that bupropion's reversal of sleepiness and REM-sleep propensity in narcolepsy may be due to blockade of dopamine or norepinephrine reuptake, and called for further investigation in placebo-controlled trials (PMID: 9614600).
- Bupropion 300 mg/day rendered a 58-year-old woman with narcolepsy-cataplexy syndrome and depression asymptomatic at 3 months, after imipramine and modafinil had failed; Epworth Sleepiness Scale score was 14 and Beck Depression Inventory score was 29 at baseline (PMID: 24930928).
- The authors of PMID 24930928 noted this was the second reported case of a depressive narcoleptic patient responding to 300 mg/day of bupropion (PMID: 24930928).

### Parkinson Disease

**Relevant studies:** 1

A single small open-label clinical evaluation of bupropion in 20 patients with idiopathic Parkinson's disease found that parkinsonism lessened by at least 30% in half the patients, suggesting mild efficacy as an anti-parkinsonian agent, though side effects were frequent and dose-limiting in five patients (PMID: 6431314). ### Alcohol Dependence

#### Literature — Alcohol Dependence

**Evidence strength:** weak, mixed
**Relevant studies:** 2

The available drug-specific evidence for bupropion in alcohol dependence is limited to preclinical animal studies. In a rat self-administration model, bupropion treatment during abstinence did not increase alcohol consumption during relapse, unlike fluoxetine which did (PMID: 31064683). In mice, bupropion dose-dependently reduced binge-like ethanol intake and chronic intermittent ethanol consumption, and subthreshold doses of bupropion combined with naltrexone also reduced binge drinking (PMID: 30817015). The evidence supports a potential role for bupropion in reducing alcohol consumption, but is based solely on animal models with no human clinical trial data in the relevant abstracts.

**Key findings:**
- In a rat alcohol self-administration model, bupropion (20 mg/kg, 14-day treatment) did not increase alcohol consumption during relapse, in contrast to fluoxetine which produced a long-lasting increase; bupropion also showed distinct effects on glutamatergic and endocannabinoid systems in prefrontal cortex compared to fluoxetine (PMID: 31064683)
- Bupropion dose-dependently blunted binge-like ethanol intake in male C57BL/6J mice using 'drinking in the dark' procedures at doses of 20 and 40 mg/kg (PMID: 30817015)
- Bupropion (20–60 mg/kg) dose-dependently reduced ethanol intake in mice following 16 weeks of intermittent access to ethanol (PMID: 30817015)
- Subthreshold doses of bupropion (20 mg/kg) combined with naltrexone (3 mg/kg) reduced binge-like ethanol intake in mice, suggesting a potential combination approach (PMID: 30817015)
- Bupropion failed to reduce consumption of a 3% sucrose solution in mice, suggesting the effect on ethanol intake may not be due to general appetite suppression (PMID: 30817015)

### Low Back Pain

#### Literature — Low Back Pain

**Evidence strength:** moderate, contradicts
**Relevant studies:** 1

The only directly relevant evidence is a randomized, placebo-controlled, 2-period crossover trial of bupropion sustained release in patients with chronic low back pain (PMID: 16202958), which found no significant difference between bupropion SR and placebo on daily pain intensity ratings, weekly pain intensity ratings, McGill Pain Questionnaire Present Pain Intensity scale, or pain relief ratings. The evidence contradicts repurposing bupropion for low back pain, at least in the non-neuropathic subtype studied (PMID: 16202958). All other abstracts were contaminated because they studied different drugs (duloxetine, ketamine, esketamine, nortriptyline, morphine, imipramine, pregabalin, diclofenac, etoricoxib, chiropractic) or studied bupropion/venlafaxine for a different therapeutic target (depression or pharmacogenetics of antidepressant response) in patients with low back pain.

**Key findings:**
- In a randomized, placebo-controlled, 2-period crossover trial of 44 patients with chronic low back pain, bupropion SR did not significantly reduce daily pain intensity ratings, weekly pain intensity ratings, McGill Pain Questionnaire Present Pain Intensity scale scores, or pain relief ratings compared to placebo (PMID: 16202958).
- Only 3 of 44 patients in the trial met criteria for neuropathic low back pain, so the negative result applies primarily to non-neuropathic chronic low back pain (PMID: 16202958).
- The authors noted that antidepressants with both noradrenergic and serotonergic effects may have greater efficacy in chronic low back pain than bupropion, which inhibits reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine but showed no evidence of efficacy in this population (PMID: 16202958).

**Contradicting PMIDs:** [16202958](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16202958/)

### Autism Spectrum Disorder

#### Literature — Autism Spectrum Disorder

**Evidence strength:** weak
**Relevant studies:** 1

Only one relevant abstract addresses bupropion in the context of autism spectrum disorder. A systematic review and meta-analysis (PMID: 39004333) examined pharmacological interventions for ADHD symptoms in individuals with ASD and found that evidence for bupropion as an intervention in this population is described as 'preliminary or nonexistent,' with only a few articles using bupropion as a comparator to methylphenidate. The review did not produce a meta-analytic estimate for bupropion specifically, leaving its efficacy and safety in ASD largely uncharacterized.

**Key findings:**
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of pharmacological interventions for ADHD symptoms in ASD found only a few articles using bupropion as an intervention compared to methylphenidate, and characterized evidence for bupropion in this population as 'preliminary or nonexistent' (PMID: 39004333).
- The same review concluded that evidence for substances such as bupropion, guanfacine, clonidine, or modafinil in ASD is either preliminary or nonexistent, in contrast to methylphenidate and atomoxetine which had sufficient data for meta-analysis (PMID: 39004333).
